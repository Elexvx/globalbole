Some 136 countries—70% of nations worldwide and 17 more than in March 2025—now report operating multi-hazard early warning systems, according to the [Global Status of Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems 2026 announcement](https://wmo.int/media/news/escalating-hazards-demand-escalated-action) released by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and the UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) at COP31 on November 12[^1]. Countries equipped with substantial to comprehensive warning architectures record disaster-related mortality rates six times lower than those with limited or moderate coverage, yet one in three states—predominantly least developed countries and small island developing states—still lack comprehensive protection[^1].

## $2 Billion Mobilized Across 160 Countries as Anticipatory Action Reaches 9.6 Million People

Since UN Secretary-General António Guterres launched the Early Warnings for All initiative in 2022 alongside WMO, UNDRR, ITU, and the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, roughly USD 2 billion has been mobilized to support around 160 countries, according to the [WMO COP31 release](https://wmo.int/media/news/escalating-hazards-demand-escalated-action)[^1]. Addressing delegates in Antalya, Guterres urged governments to treat early warnings as a permanent public service funded across the entire operational chain—from numerical forecasting and power-telecom resilience to community responders—while shifting from fragmented donor grants toward long-term country-led budgets beyond the initial 2027 milestone year[^1].

Forecast-based triggers are increasingly unlocking pre-disaster relief before extreme weather strikes: in 2025 alone, 146 anticipatory action activations across 54 countries reached 9.6 million people[^1]. Africa recorded the fastest regional improvement, bringing its average early warning score level with the Americas; within weeks of WMO declaring an El Niño in June 2026, Madagascar and Zimbabwe released pre-arranged humanitarian funds ahead of drought conditions, even though fewer than half of disaster-affected residents in Central and West Africa received timely alerts[^1].

## Strongest El Niño Since 1950 and 200 km/h Glacial Rockslide Floods Test Infrastructure Limits

WMO Secretary-General Celeste Saulo cautioned in the [November 12 briefing](https://wmo.int/media/news/escalating-hazards-demand-escalated-action) that compound hydrometeorological hazards are escalating faster than legacy protection networks and critical power-water infrastructure were engineered to handle, with 2026's strongest El Niño event since 1950 simultaneously straining national forecasting and grid-dispatch resilience worldwide[^1].

Illustrating the speed of non-linear climate hazards, Saulo pointed to an August 2026 rock-and-ice collapse along the border between Nepal and China that plunged into a river valley and generated a lethal flash flood racing at nearly 200 kilometers per hour—overwhelming downstream warning stations designed solely for seasonal monsoon floods[^1]. Because tropical cyclones, dust storms, and transboundary floods originate hundreds of kilometers from impact zones—and European weather models themselves rely on real-time observations from Africa and the Pacific—gaps in regional observation networks carry systemic risk across interconnected energy and civil infrastructure[^1].

## Staffing, Impact-Forecasting, and Telecom Bottlenecks Confront National Weather Agencies

Despite advances in low-compute artificial intelligence weather models that can run on modest workstations, the [UNDRR-WMO assessment](https://wmo.int/media/news/escalating-hazards-demand-escalated-action) identifies severe operational bottlenecks inside National Meteorological and Hydrological Services (NMHSs)[^1]. Globally, only 15 out of every 100 national weather services employ all the technical specialists required to operate and maintain their observation hardware, and just 14 out of every 100 provide impact-based forecasts translating raw meteorological variables into concrete physical damage warnings[^1].

Basic digital infrastructure remains an equally binding constraint: 29 national weather agencies still operate over unstable internet connections, hindering high-frequency satellite ingestion and redundant alert dissemination during grid outages[^1]. Sustaining early warning gains past 2027 will therefore require predictable multi-year public utility appropriations—mirroring how governments finance hospitals year after year rather than solely on opening day[^1].
