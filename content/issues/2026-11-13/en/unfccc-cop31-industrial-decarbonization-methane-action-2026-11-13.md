As negotiators gathered at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, the UN Climate Change (UNFCCC) secretariat published the [annual report of the Sharm el-Sheikh mitigation ambition and implementation work programme](https://unfccc.int/news/new-un-climate-change-report-highlights-what-it-takes-to-scale-up-industrial-climate-action) on November 12, detailing technological sequencing and investment frameworks required to move industrial decarbonization beyond isolated pilots[^1]. On the same day, UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell addressed the [Leaders-Level Non-CO₂ Greenhouse Gases Summit at COP31](https://unfccc.int/news/in-a-world-of-suffocating-disasters-action-on-methane-offers-breathing-space-un-climate-chief-at)—convened by China, the United Kingdom, and the COP30 and COP31 Presidencies—calling for rapid cuts in methane and other super pollutants while highlighting USD 54 billion in annual energy value lost to oil and gas flaring[^2].

## Yeosu and Sydney Global Dialogues Map Near-Term Electrification and Longer-Term CCUS and Green Hydrogen

Prepared under the guidance of work programme co-chairs Angela Churie Kallhauge of Sweden and Gao Xiang of China, the [UNFCCC industrial mitigation report](https://unfccc.int/news/new-un-climate-change-report-highlights-what-it-takes-to-scale-up-industrial-climate-action) synthesizes outcomes from the seventh Global Dialogue in Yeosu, Republic of Korea, in April 2026 and the eighth Global Dialogue in Sydney, Australia, in September 2026, informing CMA 8 deliberations on extending the work programme at COP31[^1].

Participants emphasized sequencing industrial abatement technologies to match national grid readiness and industrial asset structures: near-term deployment centers on energy efficiency upgrades, electrification of industrial processes, renewable power integration, fuel and feedstock switching, and alternative materials, whereas carbon capture, utilization and storage (CCUS) and green hydrogen remain pivotal for hard-to-abate heavy industries over the medium to long term[^1]. Investment-focused Pitch Hub sessions paired project developers directly with financial advisers, exemplified in Sydney by The Gambia's Mbolo Association securing European Union grant funding for a solar-powered composting facility first pitched at an earlier session[^1].

## Capturing 167 Billion Cubic Meters of Flared Gas and Unlocking 15-to-1 Economic Returns

At the Antalya leaders' summit, Stiell argued in his [official COP31 speech transcript](https://unfccc.int/news/in-a-world-of-suffocating-disasters-action-on-methane-offers-breathing-space-un-climate-chief-at) that curbing methane and other non-CO₂ super pollutants offers vital 'breathing space' to check near-term global heating and keep any overshoot of the 1.5°C threshold as brief and limited as possible alongside deep carbon dioxide reductions[^2].

Citing sector assessments and findings from UNEP and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition, Stiell noted that 70% of oil and gas methane emissions can be eliminated with existing technology—much of it at low or zero net cost—while 75% of solid-waste methane reductions save money outright because captured gas value exceeds upfront capital outlay[^2]. Globally, 167 billion cubic meters of gas are still flared each year in oil and gas production, wasting USD 54 billion in energy value, even though every dollar invested in key super-pollutant abatement generates USD 15 in economic gains across energy efficiency, crop yields, and public health[^2].

## Bridging the 0.5% Finance Bottleneck Through the Operational Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism

Despite compelling project economics, capital allocation lags starkly behind national pledges. According to [Stiell's COP31 address](https://unfccc.int/news/in-a-world-of-suffocating-disasters-action-on-methane-offers-breathing-space-un-climate-chief-at), more than 90% of Parties—accounting for roughly three quarters of global methane emissions—have now incorporated methane reduction measures into their updated Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), yet in 2023 only 0.5% of climate finance earmarked for emissions reductions went toward super pollutants[^2].

To close that implementation gap, Stiell outlined four priorities for governments and development banks: translating NDC targets into binding sectoral regulations, scaling public and private finance through multilateral development banks, building bankable project pipelines via initiatives such as the Super Pollutant Country Action Accelerator, and utilizing the newly operational UN carbon market—the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (PACM)—which has already prioritized landfill methane capture and utilization among its initial crediting methodologies[^2].
