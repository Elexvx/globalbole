Global tuberculosis (TB) deaths declined to an estimated 1.1 million in 2025 alongside 10.6 million new cases, while domestic government funding rose 14% between 2024 and 2025 to reach US$ 5.6 billion, according to the [World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2026 release](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2026-amid-challenges-countries-increase-domestic-investment-to-protect-progress-against-tuberculosis) published on November 12[^1]. That domestic budget expansion partially offset a pullback of comparable scale in international donor financing—following global health aid retrenchment by major bilateral donors including the United States—preserving essential clinical services for a disease that has seen 86 million lives saved through treatment since 2000 yet remains the world's deadliest single infectious agent[^1].

## Mortality Falls 35% Since 2015 to Hit Delayed Milestone Amid Sharp Regional Divergence

Data compiled in the [WHO Global Tuberculosis Report 2026 announcement](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2026-amid-challenges-countries-increase-domestic-investment-to-protect-progress-against-tuberculosis) show that the 35% reduction in annual TB deaths between 2015 and 2025 finally achieved the first mortality milestone of WHO's End TB Strategy, albeit five years behind schedule[^1]. By contrast, the global TB incidence rate per 100,000 population fell by only 14% over the same decade, falling well short of the 50% reduction milestone originally set for 2025[^1].

Disease burden remains heavily concentrated in 30 high-burden countries accounting for 88% of all people who developed TB in 2025, with starkly uneven regional trajectories[^1]. Between 2015 and 2025, TB incidence dropped 42% in the WHO European Region and 30% in the African Region, compared with declines of 18% in South-East Asia, 6.6% in the Eastern Mediterranean, and 0% net change in the Western Pacific; in the Region of the Americas, incidence rose for the fifth consecutive year to stand 13% above 2015 levels[^1].

## Diagnostic Gaps Leave 2.2 Million Undetected While Short-Course Drug-Resistant Regimens Surge

Across frontline care delivery, the [November 12 WHO report](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2026-amid-challenges-countries-increase-domestic-investment-to-protect-progress-against-tuberculosis) highlights persistent screening bottlenecks alongside rapid adoption of newer therapeutic protocols. Of the estimated 10.6 million individuals who fell ill with TB in 2025, 8.4 million were formally diagnosed and notified, leaving roughly 2.2 million patients either undiagnosed or unreported[^1]. Furthermore, only 59% of newly diagnosed patients were initially tested with a WHO-recommended rapid molecular diagnostic assay, trailing the 100% universal rapid-testing target established for 2027[^1].

In drug-resistant care, an estimated 370,000 people developed rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB) in 2025, with overall treatment coverage still constrained at 42%[^1]. Among RR-TB patients enrolled in care, however, uptake of the WHO-recommended six-month all-oral treatment regimen more than doubled within a single year—climbing from 18% in 2024 to 42% in 2025—demonstrating that clinical health systems can rapidly deploy shorter, higher-adherence pharmaceutical innovations once procurement channels are established[^1].

## Domestic Budgets Cushion Donor Retrenchment, Yet Global R&D Lags Below One Quarter of Target

WHO Director-General Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus noted in the [official release](https://www.who.int/news/item/12-11-2026-amid-challenges-countries-increase-domestic-investment-to-protect-progress-against-tuberculosis) that national governments have dug deeper into domestic budgets to safeguard hard-won gains amid external funding cuts, while stressing that losing over one million lives annually to a preventable and curable illness remains unacceptable[^1]. Although the US$ 5.6 billion domestic allocation cushioned immediate clinic disruptions, fiscal constraints in lower-income countries limit how fast domestic treasuries alone can expand active case-finding and molecular laboratory networks[^1].

The sharpest structural shortfall lies in biomedical research and industrial innovation: global funding for TB research stood at just US$ 1.2 billion in 2023, representing less than one quarter of the US$ 5 billion annual requirement set for 2027[^1]. Without sustained public-private R&D commitments to advance late-stage vaccine candidates, point-of-care diagnostics, and next-generation regimens, incremental service delivery gains alone will remain insufficient to bend global incidence curves onto the End TB trajectory[^1].
