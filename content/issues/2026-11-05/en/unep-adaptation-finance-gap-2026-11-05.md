## Two reporting methods, not an uncertainty band around one number

The UN Environment Programme released its Adaptation Gap Report 2026 on November 4, highlighting the continuing distance between developing countries’ adaptation needs and international public finance. Its figures for international public adaptation finance from developed to developing countries in 2024 are US$28.2 billion or US$31.7 billion, depending on the reporting method. **US$28.2–31.7 billion is not a confidence interval around a single statistic**, and the entire amount should not be described as funds already received or spent by recipients.[^1][^2]

Under the Adaptation Gap Report, or AGR, approach, based on commitments and constant 2024 prices, finance fell from US$30.4 billion to US$28.2 billion. Under the OECD approach it increased from US$30.7 billion to US$31.7 billion. Thus, an increase of about US$1 billion applies only to the latter, not to both methods. Comparing trends requires retaining the reporting method and price basis, rather than selecting the larger figure as evidence of progress.[^2]

## Compare 2035 needs on a consistent price basis

Drawing on national plans and modelling, the report’s central estimate of developing countries’ annual adaptation needs in 2035 is US$305 billion **at 2024 prices**. It mainly covers adaptation activities requiring public or blended public-private finance; private actors’ adaptation for their own needs is not fully counted. This is an estimate of future needs, not a settled annual allocation. Subtracting the 2024 financing figure directly from it would not establish a definitive gap for the same year.[^2]

Adaptation spending reduces climate impacts already occurring or difficult to avoid, through measures such as flood protection, heat-resilient infrastructure, early-warning capacity and ecosystem protection. It is a different statistical category from mitigation investment intended to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions. The international ambition discussed in the report is to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035, not to increase it by 300%, and certainly not a record of funds already delivered. Depending on the baseline, nominal annual finance in 2035 could reach US$85–120 billion.[^1][^2]

Assuming inflation of 3% a year, US$305 billion in 2024 purchasing power would correspond to approximately US$420 billion in 2035. US$85–120 billion would therefore cover around 20–29% of that year’s estimated needs. That comparison matches future finance with needs at future prices; ignoring inflation and dividing directly by US$305 billion changes what the percentages mean.[^2]

## Delivery capacity matters as well as funding totals

Finance is only one condition for implementation. Cross-sector coordination, local delivery capacity, financial management and access to projects determine whether budgets become practical protection. The report cites about US$2.8 billion in first-round applications to the loss and damage fund, compared with initial available resources of US$342 million. Applications exceeding that round’s funding demonstrate pressure on that allocation, but cannot establish a worldwide loss-and-damage financing gap, because total global needs are not yet known.[^1]

The questions that warrant continued scrutiny are how quickly finance grows, whether it reaches high-risk areas and whether implemented projects reduce losses. Models and scenarios define the limits of future needs estimates, while reporting methods determine how current amounts can be compared. Tracking verifiable allocations, implementation and outcomes is more informative than counting international fundraising ambitions as achievements already delivered.
