Serbia’s October 25 snap parliamentary election took a major turn. N1 reported Vučić’s concession at party headquarters that evening.[^1] But a concession, a partial official count and the formation of a new government are not the same event. At the time of verification, official results were still being compiled station by station. Election-night statements cannot be presented as a final allocation of seats.

The Republic Electoral Commission’s (RIK) snapshot, timestamped October 26 at 02:25 local time, showed 6,172 of 8,392 polling stations processed. “Student List—Students Win” had 1,307,404 votes and a displayed share of 49.14%; “Aleksandar Vučić—United Serbia” had 1,103,415 votes and 41.48%. The Student List led by 203,989 votes within those processed records. The denominator and coverage matter: this was neither the final result from all stations nor a final seat allocation issued by the commission.[^2]

## First, clarify Vučić’s position—and what this election decides

Vučić resigned as president in September; Assembly Speaker Ana Brnabić assumed his duties.[^3] The October 25 news therefore concerns a parliamentary defeat for the former president and the list he heads—not a presidential election defeat that day, or a fresh resignation.

The contest is for National Assembly seats. Article 100 of the current constitution provides for 250 members. The distribution of seats matters for subsequent government formation and parliamentary votes. But election night cannot bypass the counting and formation procedures to establish that one side has already taken executive power.[^4]

“Student List” is, first of all, the name of a list on the official ballot. It identifies a political grouping competing in this election; it does not establish that every candidate is personally a currently enrolled student. Assessing its prospective government personnel, specific policies or internal coordination would require separate evidence from candidate lists and programmes.

## The partial count is not yet the overall result

The RIK snapshot covered approximately 73.55% of polling stations. That figure is calculated by dividing processed stations by the total number of stations; it is not the proportion of all ballots counted. Stations vary in size, and areas not yet included need not share the voting pattern of those already processed.

The official page also showed 6,451,292 registered voters and 2,662,212 people who voted at the processed stations, explicitly limiting the latter figure to those stations. Dividing the second number by the first would not produce final national turnout. Nor would it be sound to scale up the two leading lists’ votes proportionately merely because over seven in ten stations had been processed.[^2]

This explains why a partial tally supports the statement that the Student List leads the current official count, but not a claim to final nationwide vote shares. Vučić’s concession is a separate, important political fact; official records establish the numbers station by station. The two are connected, but neither substitutes for the other.

## From polling-station records to the final national tally

At its October 25 press conference at 20:15, RIK explained the counting chain. Polling boards establish each electoral list’s votes and complete the records, then submit them to local electoral commissions. The records are scanned, statistically processed and promptly published on RIK’s website. Publication proceeds from the first processed station, rather than waiting to release every station’s results together.[^5]

There were also exceptions to the normal 20:00 closing time. Voting could continue where it had been interrupted for more than an hour, or where voters were still waiting at closing. A common national closing time therefore did not put every station at the same stage of counting at precisely the same minute.[^5]

For readers, successive publication provides early visibility into records entering the system. It also requires every citation to specify the update time and coverage. A later figure differing from an earlier one does not, by itself, establish an irregularity. What warrants verification is the particular record, the reason for any correction and the formal tally—not a political interpretation attached to every change.

## A signal of high participation is not final turnout

Independent monitor CRTA estimated turnout at 67.8% at 19:00 on October 25, with a margin of ±0.9 percentage points.[^6] This indicates high participation, but remains an observation-based estimate at a particular time—not final nationwide turnout after voting ended.

It is also a different measure from RIK’s count of voters at processed stations. The former estimates participation while voting is continuing; the latter comes from records already entered into the official system. Explaining them side by side is more useful than selecting either as a final conclusion: election-day participation and progress in establishing the vote totals are separate developments to follow.

High participation alone also cannot establish why one side prevailed. Without individual-level voter evidence, it is not possible to assume that every additional participant supported the Student List, or attribute all turnout growth to one form of mobilisation. What can be established is that the evening’s political statements and published partial official records both point to a changed competitive landscape. Explaining how that change occurred requires further research.

## What still separates a parliamentary majority from a government?

Under Article 127 of the constitution, the president consults representatives of elected lists and proposes a prime-ministerial candidate to the Assembly. The candidate presents a government programme and proposed membership. The Assembly votes on the programme, prime minister and ministers together; approval requires a majority of all members. In a 250-seat chamber, that means at least 126 votes.[^4]

The Assembly’s current Rules of Procedure further require the prime minister and ministers to take an oath before the Assembly once the government is elected.[^7] Even if the final seat distribution later gives a single list its own majority, nomination, presentation of a programme and personnel, voting and the oath remain necessary steps. An election-night lead does not automatically turn an electoral list into a government roster.

The next developments to watch are how RIK completes the remaining records and establishes the formal seat distribution, and how the winning camp translates electoral support into a government proposal capable of securing a parliamentary majority. There is no need to minimise Vučić’s concession, but a government not yet formed cannot be treated as an accomplished fact. The political landscape is changing; the institutional transition must be established through the procedures that actually follow.
