Factory-gate prices jumped in September. Household prices barely moved. Figures published on October 14 by the [National Bureau of Statistics website (stats.gov.cn)](https://www.stats.gov.cn/) put the producer price index 5.0% above a year earlier and the consumer price index 0.9% higher. Energy, metals and chemical raw materials rose sharply. Some industrial products kept getting cheaper, and the basket households actually buy rose much less. [^1][^2]

From August, the PPI rose 1.2% and the CPI 0.2%. Industrial purchasing prices were 6.9% higher than a year earlier and 1.2% higher on the month. Core CPI, which leaves out food and energy, rose 1.0% on the year and was unchanged on the month. The yearly comparison is with September 2025. The monthly one is with August 2026. Only the second measures the extra cost incurred during September itself. [^1][^2]

## Strong upstream increases do not mean every industrial price is rising

Production materials did the lifting. Their prices rose 6.5% on the year and contributed about 5.07 percentage points to the headline PPI change. Factory-gate prices of consumer goods fell 0.1%, a subtraction of about 0.03 percentage points. Inside production materials, mining prices rose 25.4%, raw-material prices 9.4% and processing prices 3.4%. The 5.0% headline is an upstream story. Factories did not all raise prices by the same amount. [^1]

What companies pay for inputs was just as uneven. Nonferrous metal materials and wires rose 20.0% on the year, fuel and power 13.6%, and chemical raw materials 12.6%. Over the month the same three rose 1.4%, 3.7% and 2.4%. Metals led the annual comparison. Fuel and power led the month. A buyer restocking soon has to treat those as separate facts. [^1]

## Household averages conceal opposing changes in spending categories

Food prices in the CPI were 1.7% lower than a year earlier. Service prices were 0.8% higher. Against August, food rose 0.4% and services fell 0.2%. The reference periods differ, so both readings can be right at once. A price still below last September can be higher than it was in August. [^2]

Transport and communications split the same way. Energy used by transport vehicles rose 14.9% on the year and 4.5% on the month. Passenger-car prices fell 1.7% annually and 0.3% monthly. Communications-device prices rose 14.3% annually and 3.6% monthly. Transport energy is a statistical category, so the change should not be relabelled as a gasoline-price change. The move in communications devices does not describe electronic products as a whole. [^2]

A household that drives to work, one that is buying a car, and one that spends a large share of its budget on food can leave the same month with very different bills. What they buy, how much, and when, decides it. The 0.9% CPI increase is not a line on every household’s spending. The index measures prices. If a family simply buys less, that change in quantity is not itself counted as a price change.

## Factory-gate, purchasing and consumer indexes use different baskets

The three indexes are built on different baskets. Under the NBS methodology, the PPI tracks the movement in industrial products’ factory-gate prices at their first sale. The purchasing-price index tracks the prices industrial companies pay for intermediate inputs. The CPI covers consumer goods and services bought by households. Transport, wholesale and retail stages may follow the factory-gate transaction, and many household services have no one-to-one equivalent in industrial output. The PPI–CPI difference is a poor stand-in for cost pass-through. [^1][^2]

The samples are wide, and they are not the same sample. Eight major categories and 268 basic classifications go into the CPI, with prices drawn from nearly 120,000 collection points in about 500 cities and counties. The factory-gate survey covers 41 industrial divisions and more than 1,300 basic product classifications. The purchasing survey covers nine major categories and more than 800 classifications. More than 40,000 enterprises are involved in the industrial price collection. The result is a set of broad, weighted statistics. It is not one firm’s purchase invoices set against its sales invoices. [^1][^2]

From January 2026 both indexes sit on a 2025 base, and the rebase changed classifications, samples and weights. The NBS puts the average effect on monthly year-on-year indexes at about 0.06 percentage points for the CPI and 0.08 percentage points for the PPI. Those are averages across months. They cannot be stripped off September’s rates to produce a supposedly “true” inflation figure. [^1][^2]

## The effect on profit depends on the individual business

Industry prices are more useful than the aggregate. In September, automotive manufacturing prices fell 2.0% on the year. Computer, communications and other electronic-equipment manufacturing prices rose 5.8%. Electricity and heat production and supply prices fell 3.9%, and nonmetallic mineral-product prices fell 2.5%. Upstream increases were not transmitted evenly to all downstream industries. An industry average is still not every constituent company’s actual transaction price. [^1]

Purchasing prices rose 6.9% and factory-gate prices rose 5.0%. The gap between those two rates is not a fall in profit margins of the same size. The mixes and the weights differ, and the indexes do not line a particular company’s inputs up against its outputs, transaction by transaction. A manufacturer that uses large amounts of metals and energy, and that cannot adjust selling prices promptly, could come under pressure if the input increases persist. A price-linked contract, inventory bought earlier, or a gain in productivity could change the outcome.

Higher selling prices do not automatically increase an upstream supplier’s profit either. Input costs, output volumes, wages, financing expenses and product mix all affect the result. Testing whether costs are squeezing profit requires company-level selling prices and costs per unit, gross margins, inventory turnover and cash flow, alongside sales volumes. Price indexes alone cannot establish whether revenue growth comes from charging more or shipping more.

## Purchasing decisions depend on exposure and pass-through conditions

For a procurement team, the useful part of the report is narrower: which of these moves match the firm’s own input mix, how often contracts reset, and whether suppliers’ quotations have already changed. Buying large inventories simply because the aggregate index is rising can create working-capital pressure, and losses if prices subsequently fall. Bringing purchases forward still depends on orders, delivery schedules and financial capacity.

Industry prices may keep rising. Companies may or may not be able to raise prices without a material loss of sales volume. Reported costs, profit and cash flow may or may not support the same picture. September’s data establish a divergence between upstream and household prices, and they suggest that some manufacturers need to watch cost pass-through. A broad recovery in manufacturing profits, a general strengthening of household demand, and an inevitable direction for future policy are not established here.
