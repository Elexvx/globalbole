The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) reported on October 29 that real gross domestic product grew at a seasonally adjusted **annualized rate** of 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026, up from 2.2% in the second quarter. The 3.4% figure does not mean “the economy actually expanded 3.4% from the second quarter to the third”: BEA also reported a non-annualized quarterly increase of approximately 0.8%. This remains an advance estimate; the next revision is scheduled for November 25 at 08:30 U.S. Eastern Time.[^1]

## The contribution from investment and final demand are different measures

The sources of growth warrant closer examination than the headline alone. BEA said investment, consumer spending and government spending increased, partly offset by a decline in exports; higher imports are a subtraction in GDP accounting. Compared with the previous quarter, faster investment growth and a return to growth in government spending were the main reasons for the acceleration. The increase in investment included private inventories and equipment, with the rise in information-processing equipment primarily related to computers and peripheral equipment. Inventory movements can significantly affect GDP in a particular quarter, but cannot by themselves demonstrate that final demand will continue expanding at the same pace.[^1]

One supplementary measure for assessing demand is “real final sales to private domestic purchasers”: personal consumption expenditures plus private fixed investment, including residential investment, but excluding inventories and government purchases.[^2] This measure grew at an annualized rate of 4.9% in the third quarter, compared with 4.6% in the second. It indicates that current private-sector demand remained relatively strong, but does not mean every industry was thriving simultaneously. Growth in consumer services involved health care and food services and accommodations, while growth in goods included items such as prescription drugs; the underlying data sources and revision risks also differ across components.[^1]

## The advance estimate, prices and the durability of growth

Price measures must not be confused with the **single-month September** inflation data released on the same day. In the quarterly GDP report, the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rose at an annualized quarterly rate of 2.1% in the third quarter, or 2.5% excluding food and energy. Neither figure is September's year-over-year increase or a forecast for the fourth quarter. This advance estimate supports the description of economic activity as resilient. Assessing whether growth can continue, however, still requires watching whether inventories retreat, whether equipment investment translates into sustained output, and the results of November's revision.[^1]
