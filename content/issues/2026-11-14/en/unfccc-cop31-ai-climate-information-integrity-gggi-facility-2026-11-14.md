Meeting at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, on November 13, the Global Initiative for Information Integrity on Climate Change — co-chaired by Brazil, the United Nations, and UNESCO — unveiled its [Priorities for Action on AI for Climate Information Integrity](https://unfccc.int/news/cop31-new-global-priorities-target-role-of-ai-in-climate-information-integrity), establishing international benchmarks for both generative model reliability and data center resource disclosure[^1]. On the same day in Doha, Qatar, Member States at the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) Plenipotentiary Conference 2026 (PP-26) [completed senior technical leadership elections across radiocommunication, standardization, and development](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-13-Directors-Election.aspx), finalizing the executive team that will steer global spectrum, satellite, and AI technical standards[^2].

## Addressing Dual AI Climate Information Gaps and Data Center Footprints

According to the [official press release distributed via UNFCCC in Antalya](https://unfccc.int/news/cop31-new-global-priorities-target-role-of-ai-in-climate-information-integrity), the newly launched Priorities tackle two interconnected dimensions of the artificial intelligence information gap: whether the public can trust climate information generated by AI systems, and whether researchers and policymakers possess verified data regarding AI's own environmental footprint[^1]. Citing warnings from the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI that generating fluent prose remains far easier than guaranteeing factual accuracy, the framework highlights acute hazards during extreme weather emergencies, when synthetic images and videos can circulate faster than official civil-defense alerts[^1].

Grounded in the Belém Declaration and the UN Global Principles for Information Integrity, the roadmap defines concrete responsibilities for governments, AI developers, advertisers, researchers, journalists, and funders[^1]. AI companies are called upon to evaluate how their models affect climate information accuracy, publish those assessments openly, and disclose electricity consumption alongside carbon, water, and land footprints across their data center infrastructure, while national governments are urged to embed information integrity directly into national AI strategies and climate action plans[^1].

## Advertising Supply-Chain Charter and EUR 2 Million German Fund Commitment

Recognizing digital advertising as the primary revenue engine of online content ecosystems, the Conscious Advertising Network (CAN) and Climate Action Against Disinformation (CAAD) — backed by the Global Initiative — concurrently launched the Charter for Climate Information Integrity in Advertising[^1]. Because opaque programmatic ad supply chains frequently prevent brands from seeing where their budgets land, the charter calls for end-to-end placement transparency while safeguarding user privacy, mandatory identity verification for all digital advertisers, stricter enforcement against greenwashing, and public disclosure by advertising agencies of commercial ties with fossil fuel companies[^1].

To convert policy principles into field capacity, Germany announced a EUR 2 million contribution to the UNESCO-administered Global Fund for Information Integrity on Climate Change, financing research grants, strategic climate communication, and public literacy initiatives[^1]. One year after information integrity entered a formal COP outcome text for the first time at COP30 in Belém, 29 countries alongside the European Union have now signed and begun implementing the Belém Declaration on Information Integrity on Climate Change[^1].

## ITU Completes Bureau Director Elections at PP-26 in Doha with Historic Representation

Parallel to content governance in Antalya, the UN agency responsible for global digital infrastructure completed its leadership ballot at the Doha Exhibition and Convention Center. As confirmed in [ITU's November 13 election announcement](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-13-Directors-Election.aspx), delegates from ITU's 194 Member States elected Eric Fournier of France — bringing more than 25 years of spectrum management and satellite coordination expertise — as Director of the Radiocommunication Bureau overseeing the international Radio Regulations treaty, re-elected Seizo Onoe of Japan (first elected in 2022 with over four decades of telecom experience) to lead the Telecommunication Standardization Bureau, and elected Nele Leosk of Estonia (with over 25 years in digital governance and technology diplomacy) as Director of the Telecommunication Development Bureau[^2].

Joining re-elected Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin of the United States and Deputy Secretary-General-elect Adel Darwish of Bahrain, Leosk's victory marks the first time since ITU's founding in 1865 that two women will serve concurrently as Elected Officials[^2]. On November 16, Member States will proceed to elect the 12-member Radio Regulations Board and the 48-seat ITU Council before PP-26 concludes on November 27, anchoring multilateral coordination across artificial intelligence, quantum networks, submarine cables, and orbital spectrum allocation[^2].
