Georgia announced approximately $4 billion and 4,500 jobs for Peach County's Middle Georgia Megasite on October 24.[^2]

Audi announced plans for its first US factory on October 25, with annual capacity up to 200,000 vehicles.[^1] The proposal brings investment, employment and industrial scale together. Its regional significance, however, depends on how those commitments become economic activity.

## From an announcement to an operating factory

Investment, employment and annual capacity cannot be added together into a single measure of economic benefit. Investment finances assets over a period; jobs refer to positions; capacity describes a potential production rate. None, by itself, establishes the sales, payroll or operating output already achieved.

Construction also has several milestones. Site preparation is different from building completion, equipment commissioning and sustained commercial production. Comparing dates therefore requires a defined milestone. Treating these stages as interchangeable can create a false impression of delay or progress.

For suppliers and potential employees, the practical significance is timing. A business considering additional equipment needs to know when orders become binding, while a worker considering training needs a recruitment window and occupational requirements. General investment intentions cannot answer those questions. The useful next disclosures will connect the overall plan to contracts, procurement, training and hiring milestones.

## Capacity is a denominator, not a sales forecast

The US Census Bureau defines full production capacity in terms of the maximum output reasonably achievable under normal, realistic conditions using installed equipment.[^3] Actual production may differ, so a nameplate figure needs to be read alongside operating assumptions.

A plant can have equipment in place without enough demand to run it fully. Conversely, demand can exceed what a new line can reliably produce during its ramp-up. Those are different constraints: selling more vehicles does not automatically solve a production bottleneck, and installing more equipment does not create customers. These are general operating risks, not claims that this project is already experiencing either problem.

The economically relevant questions include the number of shifts, the pace of commissioning, the range of products the line can build and the output that can be sustained at acceptable quality. Without those assumptions, dividing proposed capital spending by annual capacity produces only a rough ratio. It is not the production cost of a vehicle, because it omits operating expenses, asset life and the timing of expenditure.

## Factory revenue and regional value added are different

The US Bureau of Economic Analysis distinguishes gross output, broadly sales or production revenue, from value added. Value added subtracts purchased intermediate inputs and measures the contribution of labor and capital; across industries it sums to GDP.[^4]

This distinction prevents double counting. If a component supplier sells to an assembly plant and the completed vehicle is later sold, adding both sales figures does not measure the same thing as their combined contribution to GDP. A regional impact claim needs to identify whether it counts revenue, value added, wages or some combination, and whether the spending remains within the region being studied.

Sourcing decisions therefore matter. Components purchased from outside the region can support local assembly without generating the same local supplier income as purchases from a nearby manufacturer. This does not make imported inputs economically valueless; it changes where the associated activity is recorded. A factory's location alone cannot establish how much of its supply chain will be local.

Construction and continuing production also belong to different time horizons. Building work can generate activity before vehicle output starts, but that temporary phase should not be presented as an annual benefit continuing unchanged for the factory's lifetime. A credible estimate would state the years covered, geographic boundary and assumptions behind any supplier or household-spending effects.

## Announced jobs are not yet measured employment

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages counts filled jobs by workplace, including full-time, part-time, temporary and permanent positions. Its monthly employment measures refer to the pay period containing the 12th day of the month.[^5] An announcement of future positions is consequently different from an observed employment count.

The workplace basis matters locally. People can commute across county boundaries, so employment at a plant does not equal employment among residents of its host county. BLS distinguishes workplace-based job counts from household-based measures of employed people; someone holding more than one job may also be counted more than once in establishment data.[^5]

For the region, a useful assessment would track both filled positions and who can access them. Pay, required skills, shift patterns, commuting and training completion affect that access. These are questions for future hiring information, not benefits that can be inferred from the headline number. A larger job count does not alone establish household income gains or the distribution of opportunity.

## The supply chain will determine how far benefits travel

An assembly investment can create demand for parts, logistics and other services. The size of any regional spillover, however, depends on actual contracts and on whether local firms can supply the required goods and services. Multipliers are assumptions about these relationships, not extra jobs already created.

BEA's production-accounting distinction provides a useful discipline here: follow purchases and value added rather than repeatedly counting the same transaction.[^4] A supplier expansion should be assessed on its own timing and location, and temporary construction activity kept separate from ongoing factory operations. Without that separation, large totals can conceal overlapping estimates.

The announcement's significance will become clearer as commitments turn into completed facilities, sustained output and paid employment. The strongest evidence will be consistent operating and regional data, not simply a larger investment headline. That approach leaves room for substantial benefits while keeping the difference between a planned industrial opportunity and an achieved economic result visible.
