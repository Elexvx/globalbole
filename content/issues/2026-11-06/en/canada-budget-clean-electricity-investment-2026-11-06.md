## Clean electricity tax credits are extended through 2040 as total federal electrification commitments top $115 billion

Minister of Finance and National Revenue François-Philippe Champagne tabled *Budget 2026: Masters of our destiny* in the House of Commons on November 5, 2026, alongside Natural Resources Canada’s *Powering Canada Strong: From Strategy to Action* plan. The budget slows direct program spending growth from 8% to **2%**, commits to balancing day-to-day operating spending with revenues by 2027–28 (one year ahead of schedule), and aims to catalyse **$400 billion** in capital investment over six years. Through the new Productivity Mega Deduction—allowing businesses to immediately expense the full cost of most new capital investments—the marginal effective tax rate (METR) on new business investment drops from 13% to **6.4%**, which the government projects will add roughly $22 billion annually to GDP over 10 years and support up to 80,000 jobs.[^1][^2]

On grid and clean energy infrastructure, Budget 2026 commits **$52 billion** in new measures, bringing total federal electrification funding through 2041–42 to more than **$115 billion**. At an expected fiscal cost of **$38.5 billion**, the budget extends both the 30% Clean Technology Investment Tax Credit and the 15% Clean Electricity Investment Tax Credit through the end of **2040**, expands Clean Electricity ITC eligibility to high-voltage intra-provincial transmission lines in every province, and launches consultations on domestic content bonuses of up to 10 percentage points and 5 percentage points respectively. In parallel, the Canada Infrastructure Bank committed **$285 million** to the 160-kilometre, 345-kilovolt Wasoqonatl Transmission Line linking Nova Scotia and New Brunswick, projected to deliver $200 million in net present value savings to Nova Scotia electricity customers.[^1][^2]

## Affordability measures coincide with 2025 provincial accounts showing weak household consumption but surging computer investment

To address cost-of-living pressures, Budget 2026 introduces more than **$22 billion** over five years in affordability relief, including extending the temporary suspension of the federal fuel excise tax through January 2027 (saving up to 10 cents per litre on gasoline and 4 cents per litre on diesel) and providing **$4.1 billion** in 2027 for a one-time 50% boost under the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit Supplement for over 12 million Canadians (up to $339 for a single adult and $679 for a couple with two children). On the same day, Statistics Canada released its *Provincial and territorial economic accounts, 2025*, showing national real GDP grew **1.9%** in 2025 (down from 2.1% in 2024) while household spending rose just **2.0%**—the slowest pace since the 2020 pandemic contraction—and international exports edged down 1.1% amid US tariff uncertainty. By contrast, Canadian investment in computers and computer peripherals surged **14.1%** in 2025, its largest annual gain since 2011.[^1][^3]

Three methodological and accounting distinctions govern these figures. First, Budget 2026 combines accrual-basis and cash-basis multi-year commitments (for instance, $44.7 billion over 11 years to combat homelessness and $6.5 billion over six years for the forest sector are explicitly stated on a cash basis), and the $115 billion electrification figure aggregates $52 billion in new Budget 2026 measures with previously announced programs. Second, Natural Resources Canada’s projection that doubling grid capacity by 2050 could add $3 trillion to GDP represents a long-horizon modelling scenario rather than a near-term fiscal outcome. Third, Statistics Canada notes that Provincial and Territorial Economic Accounts volume estimates use a chained Fisher index formula and are only fully reconciled with national income and expenditure GDP totals during the November 30, 2026 third-quarter revision.[^1][^2][^3]
