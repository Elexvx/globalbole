On November 16, Switzerland committed CHF 30 million through its State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) in Kyiv to launch the five-year [Competitiveness for Recovery Ukraine (CORE) Programme alongside the International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/switzerland-invests-chf-30-million-ilo-supported-ukrainian-business) and the Helvetas-NIRAS consortium, targeting small-business modernization and European Union regulatory alignment with initial grant calls scheduled before year-end 2026[^1]. On the same day in Lima, Bank for International Settlements (BIS) Chief Representative for the Americas Alexandre Tombini delivered [opening remarks on heightened policy uncertainty and central bank research](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20261116-uncertainty-policymaking-and-role-research), warning that successive geopolitical, trade, and energy shocks are colliding with near-record sovereign debt across closely integrated markets[^2].

## Five-Year CORE Initiative Channels Grants Toward Ukrainian SME Modernization and European Value Chains

According to the [ILO's November 16 Kyiv announcement](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/switzerland-invests-chf-30-million-ilo-supported-ukrainian-business), the 2026-2031 CORE Programme forms part of Bern's broader CHF 5 billion long-term support pledge for Ukraine through 2036 and is co-chaired by Ukraine's Ministry of Economy and Environment alongside Switzerland[^1]. SECO Head of Economic Cooperation and Development Ambassador Pietro Lazzeri stated that boosting producer competitiveness will integrate Ukrainian firms into European and Swiss value chains, while Ukrainian Deputy Minister of Economy Oleksandr Tsybort noted that keeping enterprises producing and exporting despite attacks on energy, logistics, and industrial infrastructure anchors national recovery[^1].

Under Component IV of the programme—Productivity Ecosystems for Decent Work for SMEs in Ukraine—the ILO will work with government ministries, employers, and unions to tackle skills bottlenecks, co-finance enterprise modernization, and ensure productivity gains benefit women, veterans, displaced residents, and persons with disabilities[^1]. ILO Ukraine Country Director Aida Lindmeier and CORE Head Valeriy Dobrovolski emphasized that regional authorities, sectoral associations, and businesses sit directly on the programme's governing bodies to align grant disbursements with wartime operational priorities[^1].

## BIS Flags Faster Cross-Border Shock Transmission and Multi-Decade High Sovereign Yields

Addressing the 16th Annual BIS Consultative Council for the Americas Research Conference hosted by the Central Reserve Bank of Peru in Lima, Alexandre Tombini outlined in his [published BIS management speech](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20261116-uncertainty-policymaking-and-role-research) how sequential disruptions—the pandemic, the war in Ukraine now in its fifth year, US tariffs at century highs, renewed Middle East conflict lifting oil prices, extreme weather, and surging AI valuations—have kept policy uncertainty elevated[^2]. Citing recent empirical research, Tombini noted that foreign shocks now account for roughly half of interest-rate variation in advanced economies, far exceeding pre-late-1990s levels[^2].

Tombini warned that overlapping shocks are interacting with public debt near historical peaks at a moment when price-sensitive non-bank investors hold an expanding share of sovereign bonds[^2]. When energy shocks lift inflation and compel central banks to keep policy rates elevated, rising debt-service costs can swiftly unsettle bond markets—contributing to long-term sovereign bond yields reaching their highest levels since the turn of the century across several advanced economies, alongside capital competition and equilibrium rate expectations tied to artificial intelligence investment[^2].

## Demand-Shock Versus Supply-Shock Divergence Reshapes Central Bank Scenario Communication

Highlighting structural asymmetries in monetary policy transmission, Tombini cited [new BIS research on Latin American and advanced economies](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20261116-uncertainty-policymaking-and-role-research) showing that while uncertainty typically acts like an aggregate demand shock in advanced economies—depressing both output and inflation—it behaves like an aggregate supply shock across Latin America and emerging markets, where currency depreciation and rising risk premia push inflation higher even as output contracts[^2]. Firm-level microdata further show that irreversible investment and hiring costs induce debt-constrained businesses and households to delay capital outlays and major purchases[^2].

Elevated uncertainty has also transformed central bank communication practices: a BIS survey of 12 central banks found that nearly all respondents now deploy regular scenario analysis while shortening, conditioning, or suspending forward guidance when forecast dispersion widens[^2]. Pointing out that financial crises in Latin America and the Caribbean were roughly 70% less frequent in 2000-2016 than in the 1980s and 1990s thanks to stronger fiscal and monetary frameworks, deeper local markets, and tighter supervision, Tombini underscored rigorous policy research as a cornerstone of macroeconomic resilience[^2].
