On November 16, the COP31 Presidency of Turkiye and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) officially launched the [Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-16-COP31-Antalya-AI-Pledge.aspx) in Antalya, establishing a four-pillar roadmap to align artificial intelligence infrastructure, lifecycle procurement, and standardized footprint reporting with global climate goals through annual progress reviews up to 2030[^1]. On the same day, UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell addressed the [COP31 High-Level Green Digital Action Roundtable on Climate and AI](https://unfccc.int/news/un-climate-chief-on-artificial-intelligence-the-task-ahead-is-to-blunt-their-dangerous-edges-sharpen) to warn against off-grid fossil power generation for data centers[^3], while ITU Member States meeting at the 2026 Plenipotentiary Conference (PP-26) in Doha [elected the 48-member ITU Council and 12-member Radio Regulations Board](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-16-RRB-Council-Election.aspx) for the 2027-2030 term[^2].

## Four Action Pillars Target Clean Compute Power, Circular Hardware, and Standardized Footprint Metrics

According to the [joint press release issued in Antalya by ITU and the COP31 Presidency](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-16-COP31-Antalya-AI-Pledge.aspx), an initial coalition of national governments, technology companies, research institutes, and civil society organizations endorsed the pledge to address AI's dual role as both an accelerator of climate solutions and a source of mounting electricity and water demand[^1]. COP31 President Murat Kurum stated that industrial electrification must proceed alongside responsible AI deployment adapted to national contexts, while ITU Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin underscored the need for accountable AI innovation that reaches climate-vulnerable nations[^1].

The framework commits signatories across four operational pillars: expanding low- and zero-emission electricity for AI systems and data centers while improving water efficiency and grid integration; applying internationally recognized sustainability standards across AI design, procurement, e-waste circularity, and hardware reuse; advancing transparent environmental impact disclosures backed by ITU's international guidelines for measuring AI's full environmental footprint; and scaling financial and computing support for developing countries, least developed countries, and small island developing states, with implementation tracked annually at UNFCCC COP sessions through 2030 via Green Digital Action[^1].

## UN Climate Chief Warns Against On-Site Gas Plants and Calls for Dedicated Renewable Additions

Speaking at the Antalya roundtable, UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell cautioned in his [published remarks on artificial intelligence and climate governance](https://unfccc.int/news/un-climate-chief-on-artificial-intelligence-the-task-ahead-is-to-blunt-their-dangerous-edges-sharpen) that surging electricity demand from data centers is straining power grids and prompting local pushback over water supplies and household utility bills[^3]. Noting that some developers have turned to on-site natural gas plants to bypass grid interconnection bottlenecks, Stiell emphasized that taking fossil generation off the public grid does not remove greenhouse gases from the atmosphere and warned that shifting grid upgrade costs onto households erodes the industry's social license to operate[^3].

Stiell urged corporate boards to align capital plans with the Paris Agreement, publicly disclose energy and water consumption, add dedicated renewable generation in step with compute growth, and ensure efficiency gains translate into falling total emissions rather than deferred promises[^3]. Looking ahead to next year's G20 Presidency under the United Kingdom, where AI will be central to climate deliberations, he highlighted Tanzania's Upendo Ernest Lyimo, creator of the UN Climate Change AI for Climate Action Award-winning CLIMAID platform that combines satellite data, local records, and AI for flood and drought adaptation planning[^3].

## Doha Plenipotentiary Completes 2027-2030 Spectrum and Institutional Leadership Elections

Concurrently in Doha, Qatar, delegates attending PP-26 under conference chair Ahmad Abdulla AlMuslemani concluded voting for the UN digital agency's governing and spectrum bodies. As detailed in [ITU's November 16 Doha election announcement](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-16-RRB-Council-Election.aspx), Member States filled all 48 seats on the ITU Council for 2027-2030—allocating nine seats to the Americas, eight to Western Europe, five to Eastern Europe and Northern Asia, 13 to Africa, and 13 to Asia and Australasia—tasked with overseeing strategic policy and drafting financial plans between plenipotentiary conferences[^2].

Delegates also elected the 12 members of the independent Radio Regulations Board (RRB) across the five global regions to approve Rules of Procedure under the international Radio Regulations treaty, adjudicate frequency interference disputes, and advise World Radiocommunication Conferences on radio-frequency spectrum and satellite orbits[^2]. Together with the earlier re-election of Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin and Deputy Secretary-General Adel Darwish, alongside sector directors Eric Fournier for Radiocommunication, Seizo Onoe for Telecommunication Standardization, and Nele Leosk for Telecommunication Development, ITU's leadership is now set through 2030 as PP-26 continues until November 27[^2].
