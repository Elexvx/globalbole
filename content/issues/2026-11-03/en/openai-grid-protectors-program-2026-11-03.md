## Future campus arrangements and the Georgia contract

OpenAI launched its Grid Protectors Program on November 2, 2026, proposing to work with utilities, regulators and local policymakers so that future compute campuses can draw less electricity when power systems are under strain. The company says it will arrange for campuses to reduce electricity use when needed and, depending on local needs and available equipment, use on-site storage or generation and power controls to help manage demand. How much demand each location can cut, how quickly it can respond and how long a reduction can last still need to be agreed individually. This is therefore an arrangement for construction and operations, rather than a uniform grid service already running nationwide.[^1]

The planned campus in Effingham County, Georgia, offers a more concrete test. Georgia Power disclosed on July 22 that the project is expected to require approximately 3,200 megawatts of power. Its 25-year agreement includes **up to 1,000 megawatts** of flexible demand response, allowing the utility to reduce electricity supplied to the campus during certain periods of high demand. The utility also says OpenAI must cover the infrastructure and electric-service costs needed to serve the project, with long-term contracts and financial assurances. Approval of the contract on August 26 is a completed regulatory step. The campus remains in development, and the maximum flexible-demand commitment is not a measured reduction already achieved.[^2][^3]

## The conditions behind projected bill savings

This mechanism addresses two kinds of costs that large-load customers impose on the grid. Power lines, substations and other assets must be built and maintained even when demand is low. If compute campuses use electricity steadily when the grid has spare capacity and reliably reduce demand at peak times, their consumption may spread fixed investment costs over a larger volume of electricity sales while reducing the need to build extra generation for a small number of peak hours. Those are conditions for potential savings, not an automatic discount on household bills. Actual net benefits will depend on how contracts measure baseline consumption, how much notice is required, how long reductions can continue, how non-compliance is handled and whether reduced demand merely shifts computing work elsewhere.

Georgia Power projects that its **portfolio of large-load agreements and expected growth** could save customers approximately $950 million annually from 2029. It estimates savings of at least $15 a month for a typical residential customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours a month. These are neither benefits already delivered by the single OpenAI campus nor a reduction in current bills. Our editorial assessment is that the program matters because it moves the social costs of AI infrastructure from broad promises toward obligations that can be contracted, dispatched and audited. The next test should be metering records and regulatory disclosures after the campus begins operating, rather than the maximum megawatt figure announced in advance.[^1][^3]
