On November 12, 2026, [the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) officially released the 50th edition of its flagship 423-page report, International Migration Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-observer-roundtable-on-small-and-medium-sized-enterprises_7b7e35d4-en.html), holding a joint launch webinar with the University of Oxford's Centre on Migration, Policy and Society (COMPAS) and the Migration Observatory to examine evolving cross-border migration flows and labor market integration across OECD economies[^1][^2]. Simultaneously, [the International Labour Organization (ILO) detailed the financial outcomes of its high-level Geneva partners' meeting co-convened with Qatar and the Netherlands](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-and-partners-mobilize-support-palestinian-jobs-and-recovery), announcing a €5 million pledge from Italy that brings total mobilized funding over the past year to roughly US$9 million toward the 2026–2029 Decent Work and Recovery Programme (DWRP)[^3].

## 50th Edition of the OECD International Migration Outlook: Demographic Pressures and Skills Integration

According to [the OECD launch announcement and publication registry](https://www.oecd.org/en/events/2026/11/launch-of-international-migration-outlook-2026.html), the 423-page 50th anniversary edition of *International Migration Outlook 2026* provides comparative data on permanent and temporary migration flows, labor market outcomes of foreign-born workers, and policy reforms across OECD member states[^1][^2]. Presenting the findings alongside Dr. Madeleine Sumption (Migration Observatory, COMPAS) and Jill Rutter (Institute for Government), OECD Acting Director for Employment, Labour and Social Affairs Mark Pearson highlighted the tension between tightening visa rules in several OECD countries and persistent structural labor shortages in healthcare, construction, digital engineering, and clean-energy transition sectors[^1].

Across both the flagship outlook and [upcoming regional analyses such as Labor Migration in Asia scheduled for November 26](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/forthcoming.html), the OECD emphasized that maximizing the economic contribution of migration requires faster recognition of foreign qualifications, targeted language and digital upskilling, and stronger labor-market inclusion for accompanying family members and refugees[^1][^2].

## 252,000 Jobs Lost in Gaza and $2.77B Income Collapse: ILO Outlines $50M Priority Recovery Portfolio

While OECD economies calibrate skilled migration channels, acute conflict-driven labor collapse was the focus of the ILO's Geneva donor mobilization. According to [the ILO's post-meeting report](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-and-partners-mobilize-support-palestinian-jobs-and-recovery), between the third quarters of 2023 and 2025 Gaza lost 252,000 jobs, pushing the unemployment rate to 85.1% and inflicting US$2.77 billion in cumulative labor income losses, while unemployment in the West Bank stood at 27.9% in the second quarter of 2026 amid movement restrictions and economic contraction[^3].

At the Geneva meeting convened by ILO Director-General Gilbert F. Houngbo, [Italy pledged €5 million toward the Decent Work and Recovery Programme (DWRP) 2026–2029, bringing total resources mobilized over the past year—including US$700,000 from ILO internal funds and partner commitments—to approximately US$9 million](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/ilo-and-partners-mobilize-support-palestinian-jobs-and-recovery)[^3]. Building on prior ILO emergency interventions that generated roughly 130,000 paid workdays and supported rights-based social allowances for more than 30,000 older persons and persons with disabilities, the new DWRP identifies approximately US$50 million in basic investment needs across 10 priority project proposals spanning enterprise recovery, social protection, and labor governance[^3]. Palestinian Labour Minister Enas Dahadha Attari, Qatari Labour Minister Ali bin Samikh Al Marri, and Dutch Ambassador Erica Schouten urged international donors to close the remaining US$41 million funding gap to restore livelihoods and institutional capacity[^3].
