US factories, mines and utilities produced more in September. The month looked stronger than the quarter. The Federal Reserve reported on October 16 that seasonally adjusted industrial output rose 0.7%, after a revised 0.1% increase in August, and was 2.2% above a year earlier. Manufacturing rose 0.3%. Mining increased 0.2%. Electric and gas utilities grew 3.5%. The total production index stood at 104.0, with the 2017 average set to 100.[^1]

Stretch the comparison across quarters and the pace slows. Total industrial output grew at a 2.8% annual rate in the third quarter, down from 4.8% in the second. Manufacturing’s corresponding rate slowed from 5.6% to 0.7%. September can have grown faster than August even while the third quarter grew more slowly than the second. An annualized quarterly rate takes the change from the previous quarter and states it as an annual pace. Set it beside the monthly changes, and keep the two apart. It describes pace. It is not the actual outcome of the year ahead.[^2]

## A stronger total conceals substantial differences between industries

Utilities carried September. Output rose 3.5%, with increases in both electricity and natural gas. Over the third quarter the sector expanded at a 14.8% annual rate. Mining increased 0.2% in the month and at a 5.0% annual rate in the quarter. Manufacturing was back to monthly growth, and the increase was markedly smaller than utilities’.[^1]

Weights decide how far any of that moves the total. Industrial production uses value-added-based weights, so utilities’ contribution depends on both its weight and the change in its output. The index measures real production. Household electricity bills and the price a business pays for natural gas are separate price indicators.[^4]

Durables barely moved: output increased just 0.1% in September. Primary metals, machinery, and electrical equipment, appliances and components rose 1.6%, 1.4% and 1.3%, respectively. Motor vehicles and parts fell 3.0%. Nondurable manufacturing rose 0.4%, including a 1.9% increase in printing and support activities and a 1.0% rise in chemicals. A recovering aggregate does not spare anyone the job of looking at a particular supply chain.[^2]

## Semiconductor strength does not mean all equipment is expanding together

Durable manufacturing grew at a 3.8% annual rate in the third quarter. Semiconductors and related electronic components expanded at a 28.4% annual rate, one of the drivers the Federal Reserve identifies. Nondurable manufacturing fell at a 1.6% annual rate. Semiconductor and related component output rose 2.1% in September and was 19.9% higher than a year earlier. Computers and peripheral equipment fell 0.8% in that same month.[^1][^3]

Production in the semiconductor industry is growing rapidly. That is the conclusion the figures will support. They do not show demand accelerating for all technology hardware, and they do not identify artificial intelligence as the cause. The report does not break chip orders down by end use. It does not say how much of the growth came from data centers, consumer electronics or other customers.

Classified by who uses the product, business equipment production was unchanged in September. It grew at a 6.6% annual rate in the third quarter, down from 14.6% in the second. Transit equipment fell 1.7% in the month. Information processing equipment declined 0.9%. Industrial and other equipment rose 1.3%. Industry classifications name the producing sector. Market classifications name the use. The two overlap, and each supplies what the other leaves out.[^2]

Consumer goods rose 0.7% in September. Nondurables were up 1.2% and durables down 1.5%. Total consumer goods output was still 0.1% below its year-earlier level. Construction supplies rose 1.0% on the month. Materials increased 0.7%. These rates are production. They are not growth rates for retail sales, for what consumers actually spent, or for construction activity.[^2]

## Higher utilization does not mean industry is running at full capacity

Capacity in use rose to 76.9% in September for industry as a whole, 2.5 percentage points below the 1972–2025 average. Manufacturing utilization increased 0.1 percentage point, to 75.9%, and that was 2.3 points below its own long-run average. Mining reached 87.5%, 2.3 points above its historical average. Utilities utilization rose 2.3 percentage points, to 73.8%, and remained substantially below its own average.[^1]

The denominator is not the accounting value of business assets. It is the Federal Reserve’s estimate of sustainable maximum output: what existing equipment can keep producing on a realistic work schedule, allowing for normal downtime and sufficient inputs. Utilization divides the seasonally adjusted production index by the corresponding capacity index. Output can move it. So can capacity.[^4]

A long-run average is a historical reference, and no more. Whether one business can supply more still depends on its equipment, its staff and its intermediate inputs, and on whether those are available in time. Mining is already above its own average. Manufacturing is still below its. The constraints inside US industry are not all of one kind.

## Real output estimates are still subject to revision

G.17 covers manufacturing, mining, and electric and gas utilities. The whole economy is outside its scope. Its broad manufacturing definition includes industries classified as manufacturing under the North American Industry Classification System, and it also includes logging and newspaper, periodical, book and directory publishing, which have historically been counted in. Exclusively online publishing is left out. The manufacturing figures here follow that broad definition throughout. They should not be mixed with the narrower manufacturing row in the tables.[^1]

Factories do not report a physical quantity for every series. Where it is appropriate, the Federal Reserve uses product data such as tons of steel and barrels of oil. Where a suitable physical indicator is unavailable, output is estimated from inputs such as production-worker hours. Value-added weights then give a change in a large industry a different effect on the total from the same change in a small one.[^4]

September’s figures are preliminary. The first estimate, the methodology says, is revised in each of the following five months as further data arrive. Published growth rates are calculated from unrounded indexes. A result worked out from indexes displayed to one decimal place can differ, and the difference is not in itself an error. This release revised August’s total output change from no growth to a 0.1% increase, and manufacturing from a 0.3% decline to a 0.2% decline.[^1][^4]

Table 15 puts numbers on the revision risk. The 15th- and 85th-percentile reference estimates for September’s monthly change in total output are 0.28% and 0.98%. For manufacturing they are −0.08% and 0.55%. The estimates use historical revisions since 2008, excluding April through November 2025, and they indicate the possible range after the fifth monthly revision. They are not forecasts of production in later months, and they are not guaranteed upper and lower bounds. Manufacturing’s range crosses zero. A modest preliminary increase should not be treated as a conclusion that cannot change.[^4]

## Businesses need to know whether orders keep pace

Higher production helps spread fixed costs only if the orders hold up, if existing plants can run equipment harder, and if the extra revenue covers the extra labor, energy and materials. Should the additional goods mainly accumulate as inventory, or should input costs rise faster, profits may not improve. G.17 does not provide company inventories, sales contracts or margins. More output is not, by that fact, more earnings.

Motor vehicles and parts fell 3.0% on the month. For that chain, that monthly fall is the relevant figure, ahead of the 0.7% rise in national output. What it does to any supplier still depends on customers, products and how inventory is held. Semiconductor suppliers have the stronger production signal, and they still need the orders to last, the product mix to match what they ship, and the deliveries to happen. Utilities, before anyone judges revenue and cost, also need electricity and gas selling prices and the composition of customer demand. This production report does not attribute those changes to specific causes.

Whether output rises again in October is only part of the next test. The rest is whether manufacturing gains reach more industries, whether business equipment returns to monthly growth, and whether the decline in vehicles continues. The next monthly release is scheduled for November 17. An annual revision is set for November 24. It will change the index base year to 2022, incorporate manufacturing benchmark data for 2023 and 2024, and update monthly indicators, seasonal factors and capacity information. Methods for some series may change as well.[^1][^4]

The clearest signal in September is a relatively strong monthly increase in industrial production, beside a quarterly manufacturing expansion that was modest and uneven. Later months, company orders and revised figures have to show whether that turns into a production recovery with more staying power.
