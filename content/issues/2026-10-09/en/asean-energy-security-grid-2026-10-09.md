Fuel supplies can be interrupted now. Electricity demand will keep growing for years. ASEAN is trying to deal with both at once. A joint statement from the 44th ASEAN Ministers on Energy Meeting, published on October 8, placed reliable supply, regional connectivity, investment and the energy transition on a shared agenda. The meeting itself was held on October 7 in Pasay, Metro Manila, in the Philippines. The ninth ASEAN Energy Outlook, published and launched on October 8, puts regional cooperation at the centre of efforts to make the energy system less vulnerable.[^1][^2]

## Pathways rather than firm predictions

The ASEAN Centre for Energy’s report uses historical data from 2005 to 2024 and extends the analysis to 2060. Three scenarios sit side by side. In one, current trends continue. In another, countries deliver the national targets they have stated. In the third, the transition is more coordinated across the region. The centre describes internally consistent pathways under particular assumptions. These are not firm predictions of future outcomes. Cross-border electricity trade, efficiency and investment cooperation are what separate the coordinated scenario from countries pursuing their policies on their own.[^2]

An interconnector does more than add a transmission route. It lets markets share sources of electricity, and share the resources that keep supply and demand in balance. A combination that looks inexpensive in a model still has to be built, traded and financed. Regional cooperation widens the options. Individual countries still have to deliver the infrastructure.

## The 2030 targets were agreed last year

Renewables supplying 30% of total primary energy, and 45% of installed generating capacity, are not new pledges from this meeting. Ministers endorsed them on October 16, 2025, alongside a 40% reduction in energy intensity relative to 2005, under the ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation 2026–2030. Primary energy supply includes uses outside electricity. Installed capacity measures equipment. Neither figure can stand in for renewables’ share of the electricity that is actually generated.[^3]

The new outlook puts a build rate on that capacity goal. Reaching the 45% renewable-capacity target in 2030 requires roughly 26 gigawatts of renewable additions annually, about four times the pace of the past decade. In the coordinated-transition scenario, cross-border interconnection grows approximately twentyfold by 2060, and electricity exchanged between the grid’s sub-regions grows about twelvefold. Those are changes inside a modelled pathway. They are not approved projects, and the financing has not been secured.[^2]

## Oil reserves offer a separate buffer

Grids and renewable plants are medium- and long-term construction. A break in fuel supply needs a faster buffer. Reuters reported on October 8 that Philippine Energy Secretary Sharon Garin said the country planned to establish a national oil reserve by the end of 2027, potentially converting it into a regional stockpile later. Whether to host regional inventories, and how to set sales terms, would remain decisions for the countries concerned. This is an announced plan, not a completed storage facility.[^4]

A reserve and a shift away from oil do different jobs. More inventory can buy time during a short disruption. Efficiency and electrification can gradually reduce oil demand in some sectors. A cleaner electricity mix does not, by itself, change transport and the other uses of fuel. Those changes take time, so the two efforts have to proceed together.

## Turning shared goals into a working regional market

The meeting’s agenda and the outlook treat energy security and lower emissions as two parts of the same investment programme. Progress would mean connecting new renewable projects, running cross-border electricity trade that operators can rely on, and turning reserve plans into stocks that can actually be dispatched.

Coordination has practical limits. Connecting more sources of electricity requires decisions on transmission allocation, on supply responsibilities and on cost sharing. Expanding oil storage involves procurement, stock rotation and the conditions for emergency access. Those details decide whether a shared commitment helps when prices swing or a supply is disrupted. What October produced is ASEAN’s effort to turn goals already on the books, including the 2030 percentages, into coordinated action.
