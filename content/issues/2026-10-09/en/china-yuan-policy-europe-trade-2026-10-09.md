The People’s Bank of China published its position on the yuan on October 8. It reiterated that China does not pursue competitive devaluation to gain a trade advantage, and it announced additional foreign-exchange-related reporting to the International Monetary Fund from 2027. The statement arrived as Europe’s debate over China’s trade surplus and the currency grew sharper. It clarified policy principles and left room for the yuan to move in either direction. It did not set out a path of future appreciation or depreciation.[^1][^2]

## No exchange-rate target, with room to address disruption

The statement describes China’s system as a managed float based on market supply and demand, with reference to a basket of currencies. It emphasizes the market’s decisive role. The central bank says it sets no target exchange-rate level, does not intervene in the long-term trend, and withdrew from routine foreign-exchange intervention after 2017.[^1]

Financial-stability tools remain available. Major external shocks can still prompt macroprudential tools and efforts to guide expectations, and direct intervention is possible in extreme circumstances. The stated focus is preventing severe short-term movements, especially rapid depreciation, from undermining stability. Reading “no target” as the absence of all policy influence misses that qualification. Reading measures against overshooting as a guarantee of any particular price goes beyond the statement as well.[^1]

The bank says the yuan has appreciated about 9% against the dollar since the start of 2025. Reuters, on the same day, reported a gain of about 4% since the start of 2026. Those are different starting points, not two readings of one period, and they do not contradict each other. The bank also cautioned that forces favoring both appreciation and depreciation remain, leaving the outlook uncertain.[^1][^2]

## European concerns and China’s explanation remain apart

EU trade chief Maroš Šefčovič was in Beijing to discuss reducing the bloc’s trade deficit with China, Reuters reported. Citing EU data, it put that deficit at €360.6 billion in 2025, up 15% from the previous year. European policy debate links the yuan’s valuation to pressure from import competition. China’s central bank emphasizes industrial competitiveness instead, and rejects reducing other economies’ structural problems to exchange rates.[^2]

One exchange-rate figure will not settle the disagreement. A bilateral nominal rate is simply the price of one currency in another. An effective rate follows a basket of trading partners. A real rate takes relative prices into account as well. A stronger yuan against the dollar cannot, on its own, show how competitiveness against European goods has changed, and it cannot validate or invalidate every valuation model.

The statement rejects treating individual model estimates as definitive evidence of yuan undervaluation. It points to capital flows, expectations, monetary policy and risk events as other influences. This is the central bank’s policy interpretation of the dispute, distinct from assessments by market institutions or European officials. Trade imbalances still have to be explained with demand, industrial structure and cross-border capital movements in view. Currency changes are not the sole cause, whatever valuation method is used.[^1]

## More data reporting provides a concrete next step

Additional foreign-exchange reporting to the IMF from 2027 is narrower than a statement of principle, and it comes with a date. The document notes that China has published balance-of-payments and related foreign-exchange data under the IMF’s Special Data Dissemination Standard since 2016. It does not itemize the new reporting categories. What additional information becomes available will depend on subsequent disclosures.[^1]

Greater transparency could give participants a more common body of evidence when they argue about currency policy. It does not automatically change tariffs, and it does not establish that China and the EU have reached a trade agreement. The bank also calls for expanding domestic demand, improving the business environment and advancing openness during the 2026–2030 five-year plan. For a durable rebalancing of trade, the implementation of those domestic policies will matter, as will concrete outcomes from China–EU discussions.[^1]

For now, the statement draws a boundary. Market pricing and movement in both directions sit alongside tools for short-term disorder. What happens after that depends on the reporting, on domestic demand and on the negotiations. The position itself is not a precommitted path for the exchange rate.
