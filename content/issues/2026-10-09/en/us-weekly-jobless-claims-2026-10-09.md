New claims for US unemployment benefits remain relatively low. The Labor Department reported on October 8 that seasonally adjusted initial claims totaled 197,000 in the week ending October 3, down 2,000 from the previous week’s revised level. Continuing claims rose, in an earlier reference week. Layoffs, ongoing receipt of benefits and hiring are different measurements. A fall in new claims is not evidence that the whole labor market has strengthened markedly.[^1]

## Initial claims fall against a revised baseline

The preceding week’s figure was revised from 197,000 to 199,000, so the reported decline of 2,000 is measured against that revised baseline. Line up only the first estimates in successive releases and the figures look unchanged. The four-week moving average, which smooths weekly fluctuations, fell to 198,000, down 2,500 from the preceding week’s revised average.[^1]

Only a narrow reading follows. New unemployment pressure, as it shows up in benefit applications, remains relatively low. Initial claims arrive frequently and are sensitive to changes in layoffs. They cover people entering the unemployment-insurance application process, not everyone who loses work. They do not directly count hiring. Low claims can accompany expanding recruitment. They can also occur when employers are reluctant both to dismiss workers and to add staff.

The latest reading should not be described as a new historical record. The series in the same Labor Department report includes 189,000 initial claims for the week ending July 18, 2026, below the latest 197,000. “Remaining low” describes the evidence more accurately.[^1]

## The rise in continuing claims relates to September 26

Continuing claims refer to a week earlier than initial claims. Seasonally adjusted insured unemployment totaled 1,716,000 in the week ending September 26, up 17,000 from a revised 1,699,000. The insured unemployment rate held at 1.1%. These are not figures for the week ending October 3. The rate is not the unemployment rate for the entire labor force.[^1]

A slightly longer window did not move the same way. The four-week average for insured unemployment fell by 12,250 to 1,711,000 from the previous revised average. A weekly increase can sit alongside a falling moving average, which is why one week’s change cannot establish a sustained deterioration. The latest figures are also advance estimates and remain subject to revision.[^1]

Continuing claims help track people still receiving unemployment insurance. They are also affected by new entrants, by re-employment and by the end of benefit eligibility. Showing that unemployed people generally need longer to find work would require a sustained series and other survey evidence. This increase alone does not demonstrate longer job searches.

## Hiring momentum requires hiring data

The Bureau of Labor Statistics’ August Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, released September 29, provides another view. Job openings stood at about 7.1 million, hires at 5.2 million, and layoffs and discharges at 1.6 million, all little changed from the preceding month. Openings measure positions available at month-end. Hiring and separations measure flows throughout the month. Neither can simply be added to a week of initial claims, or compared in scale with one.[^2]

That monthly survey predates the latest claims figures. It cannot establish that recruitment improved in October. What it shows is that neither hiring nor layoffs accelerated significantly during its own reference period. The latest decline in initial claims primarily eases concern about layoffs spreading rapidly. Whether job opportunities are expanding, and whether labor-market turnover is becoming more active, depends on subsequent reports on hiring, vacancies and employment more broadly.

Fewer layoffs and more hires illuminate different parts of economic resilience. Revisions, reference periods and coverage have to be read together. Otherwise a modest weekly report is turned into a claim of broad prosperity, or a one-week rebound in continuing claims is turned into a turning point for the labor market.
