## COP31 Opens in Antalya: January–September Warming Hits 1.47°C Amid Record Greenhouse Gases and 400-Gigaton Glacier Loss

As the 31st UN Climate Change Conference (COP31, November 9–20, 2026) opened in Antalya, Türkiye, on November 9, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) released its flagship State of the Climate Update for COP31[^1][^2]. Synthesizing nine international datasets, the report shows that the global mean near-surface temperature from January to September 2026 stood at 1.47°C (±0.13°C) above the 1850–1900 pre-industrial baseline—making 2023 through 2026 the four warmest years on record and the past 12 years the warmest 12-year stretch ever observed (following 1.55°C in 2024, 1.46°C in 2023, and 1.43°C in 2025)[^1]. WMO further warned that a super El Niño that began strengthening in August 2026 is projected to peak in December as one of the strongest on record, carrying warming momentum into early 2027[^1].

Physical climate indicators across the atmosphere, cryosphere, and hydrological cycle reached new extremes. Global atmospheric concentrations in 2025 hit 426.8 ppm for carbon dioxide (up 53% from 278 ppm in 1750), 1,948 ppb for methane (up 167% from 729 ppb), and 339.1 ppb for nitrous oxide (up 26%), with real-time measurements confirming continued increases in 2026[^1][^2]. Glaciers worldwide lost more than 400 gigatons of ice in 2025—equivalent to roughly 1 millimeter of global sea-level rise or five Olympic swimming pools per second—marking the fourth consecutive year in which all 19 global glacier regions recorded net mass loss, while 42% of assessed global land area experienced below-normal terrestrial water storage (compared with a 25% long-term average)[^1]. Severe climate impacts in 2026 included more than 30,000 excess heat deaths in Europe, 146.5 million people exposed to extreme April heatwaves in India, and over 1,300 fatalities in Nepal following a late-August Himalayan glacial collapse and flash floods; meanwhile, the number of countries operating Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems (MHEWS) more than doubled from 56 in 2015 to 136 in 2025[^1].

## US DOE Order No. 202-25-1F Takes Effect in Puerto Rico With Mandatory December 8 Generator Maintenance Accounting

Rising heat stress and aging thermal infrastructure continue to test vulnerable island and regional power grids. Under Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Order No. 202-25-1F took effect on November 8, 2026, through February 5, 2027, authorizing the Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority (PREPA) to dispatch specified generation units listed in Attachment A whenever reserve margins are threatened, extending emergency authorities previously issued in May, August, and October 2026[^3].

Crucially, Order No. 202-25-1F pairs emergency dispatch flexibility with strict mechanical integrity safeguards to prevent emergency runs from causing permanent generator breakdowns. The order stipulates that no emergency operation may compromise constraints necessary to protect physical equipment integrity, and directs PREPA to submit a comprehensive written accounting to the Department of Energy by December 8, 2026, detailing each covered unit's manufacturer-recommended maintenance intervals, cumulative operating hours since last maintenance, and completed, ongoing, and scheduled maintenance timelines[^3]. Together, the WMO climate assessment at COP31 and the DOE reliability mandate in Puerto Rico underscore the dual imperative facing global energy planners: accelerating emissions reductions while enforcing disciplined engineering maintenance across climate-stressed power grids[^1][^3].
