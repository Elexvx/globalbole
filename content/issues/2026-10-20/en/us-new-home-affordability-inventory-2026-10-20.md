NAHB reported October 19 that October builder confidence fell three points to 29, with 43% of builders cutting prices.[^1] The immediate analytical question is not simply whether a seller is willing to discount. It is whether the purchase can fit a household’s cash flow, and whether the home can be delivered when it is needed.

The latest August new-home sales release from the Census Bureau and HUD provides a separate statistical backdrop: sales at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 684,000, 483,000 homes for sale at month-end and 8.5 months of supply. The median sale price was $393,700. Those are August estimates published September 24, not measurements of October activity.[^2]

## The monthly payment is not the sticker price

A buyer finances a loan balance rather than a headline discount. Principal, interest rate and repayment term jointly determine principal-and-interest payments. The CFPB’s Loan Estimate explanation also separates that payment from the estimated total monthly payment, which can include taxes and insurance. An unchanged loan payment does not mean that the household’s total housing outlay is unchanged.[^3]

A hypothetical comparison makes the distinction concrete. For a $400,000 home with a 20% down payment, a $320,000 fully amortizing, 30-year fixed-rate loan at 6% requires about $1,919 a month in principal and interest. At 7%, the same loan requires about $2,129. If the home price instead falls 5% to $380,000 and the down-payment proportion stays at 20%, the $304,000 loan at 7% still requires about $2,023 a month.

Thus, in this example, a price cut does not fully offset the rate increase. The figures are illustrative calculations, not current offers or a forecast; they exclude taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, fees and association charges. Actual offers also depend on loan characteristics and borrower circumstances. The point is narrower: a price comparison that does not hold financing assumptions explicit cannot establish a payment comparison.

## Incentives shift costs across time

The CFPB explains that interest-linked discount points exchange a higher upfront payment for a lower rate, while interest-linked lender credits generally reduce upfront closing costs in exchange for a higher rate. It also notes that some credits are unrelated to the rate, including promotional or compensatory credits. Their label alone does not reveal their economics.[^4]

A builder’s price reduction, a closing-cost contribution and a financing concession therefore should not be added together as though they were identical dollars of permanent saving. One changes the purchase amount; another changes cash due at closing; a rate concession changes payments according to its precise terms. The relevant comparison is the full written offer, including whether an advertised advantage expires, rather than the size of the promotion alone.

## The inventory contains several different clocks

The August release divides seasonally adjusted for-sale inventory into 114,000 homes not started, 256,000 under construction and 113,000 completed. Completed homes therefore account for roughly 23.4% of the 483,000 total, calculated from the published rounded estimates.[^2] The entire stock cannot be read as a count of homes ready for immediate occupancy.

Census definitions distinguish a permitted but not-started home from one being built and one completed. A new house can also be counted as sold when a contract is signed or a deposit accepted, before construction starts. The sales survey does not follow the transaction through final closing.[^5] Reported sales consequently do not equal completed homes handed over to buyers during the month.

The implications differ by stage. A not-started unit can represent future supply but cannot solve an immediate move-in deadline. A unit under construction brings delivery timing into the comparison. A completed unsold unit is further along physically, but its location, price, condition and contract still determine whether it suits a buyer. These are economic interpretations of the categories, not evidence that every builder faces the same pressure or that every completed home will be discounted.

## Months of supply is a ratio, not a deadline

Census defines months of supply by relating for-sale stock to the current sales pace, assuming no additional homes enter that stock.[^5] The August 8.5-month figure is therefore not a prediction that a particular house will take 8.5 months to sell. Nor does it mean all inventory can be delivered over that period.

The denominator matters. With the same stock, a slower sales pace mechanically raises the ratio; a faster pace lowers it. A change in months of supply can therefore reflect demand as well as construction. Evaluating the ratio alongside its numerator and denominator avoids describing every increase as a construction glut or every decrease as a recovery.

The release’s 684,000 sales figure is an annualized, seasonally adjusted pace, not the number of homes sold in August. Its unadjusted estimate for the actual month is 57,000.[^2] Mixing annualized sales with monthly counts or differently adjusted inventory would produce a misleading comparison even if each number were copied correctly.

## A monthly move is not yet a durable turn

August’s reported sales increase from July was 6.4%, with a 90% confidence interval of plus or minus 19.5 percentage points. Because that interval includes zero, the release does not establish a statistically significant increase. Preliminary figures can be revised; Census says several months are needed to establish a sales trend.[^2]

The median price also needs care. It is the middle transaction price, not a constant-quality price index. The release notes that price statistics reflect changes in the regional and size composition of homes sold as well as price changes for similar homes. A lower median can therefore arise partly because a different mix of properties sold, rather than because every comparable house became cheaper.[^2]

The September sales report is scheduled for October 27.[^2] Until that release and subsequent revisions add evidence, the stronger conclusion is about the structure of the problem: purchasing power depends on recurring payments and upfront cash, while usable supply depends on construction stage and location. A confidence reading, a discount and an inventory total each illuminate part of that picture. None alone proves that the affordability constraint has eased.
