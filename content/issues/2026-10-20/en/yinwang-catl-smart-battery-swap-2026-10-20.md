NBD reported on October 19 that Yinwang, CATL and CAES would connect Qiankun smart-driving technology with the Choco-Swap network.[^1]

The important question is whether vehicle software and energy-replenishment infrastructure can work together in practice. Evaluating such a collaboration requires more than timing a single mechanical operation. Drivers need to know whether they can find a compatible station before setting off, whether a usable battery will be available on arrival, and who will resolve a fault. Signing a cooperation agreement and providing a service that motorists can use every day are different stages.

## Define what the stopwatch measures

In April 2026, CATL advertised 99-second replenishment for the Choco-Swap version of the ARCFOX S3.[^2] No independent test evidence was obtained for this article, and this company claim does not establish that every vehicle at every station can complete the entire replenishment journey in that time.

A meaningful assessment should separately record the drive to the station, waiting, equipment operation and departure. It should also disclose the vehicle tested, the start and finish conditions, and any human intervention. Reporting only the equipment’s operating time leaves readers unable to judge the total time required during busy periods. For fleets with orders to complete on schedule, predictability may matter as much as the fastest recorded swap. These are proposed ways to evaluate the service, not operating results published by the three companies.

## Finding a station is not the same as finding an available battery

One difficulty is matching demand from vehicles on the road with the batteries available at stations. The review by Kumbhar and Kalkhambkar treats vehicle location and charge level, queues, charged-battery stocks and charging schedules as parts of a connected operational problem.[^3] After a vehicle takes a charged battery away, the returned battery still needs recharging. Idle swapping machinery does not automatically mean that a suitable battery is immediately available for the next vehicle.

A station displayed on a navigation screen is therefore several steps short of a completed service. At a minimum, the system should explain how recently inventory information was updated, whether a reservation actually sets resources aside, how late arrivals are handled, and whether it can recommend a reliable alternative when batteries run short. Otherwise, giving more drivers the same recommendation may simply send them to a busy station at the same time. These operational questions require evidence; calling a system smart does not answer them.

## Connected data cannot replace hardware compatibility

CATL introduced standardized No. 20 and No. 25 swappable battery blocks in December 2024.[^4] That provides background on its approach to standardization, but publishing a specification does not make it usable by every new-energy vehicle.

Alhazmi’s open-access review explains that compatibility involves battery dimensions, voltage, communications, thermal management and mechanical locking, rather than appearance or the name of an interface alone.[^5] The practical implication is that station recognition by vehicle software, physical battery removal and installation at the chassis, and checks performed by station equipment must each be verified. Buyers should consult compatibility lists for the specific vehicle model and battery version. A brand’s participation in a partnership does not give its entire range battery-swapping capability.

## Better scheduling still needs cost and safety limits

The research also examines trade-offs in centralized charging and inventory: operators need enough batteries to meet demand while accounting for charging costs and battery degradation.[^3] The economics of swapping therefore extend beyond the fee for an individual operation. Drivers need to compare rental and service charges, while operators must fund stations, equipment and spare batteries. Whether this collaboration can lower those costs remains a question for actual products and operating data.

As batteries repeatedly circulate between vehicles and stations, quality records become more important. Alhazmi’s review identifies tracking battery history and health as part of lifecycle management.[^5] For users, clear quality commitments, anomaly detection, fault resolution and accessible dispute channels help turn technical capabilities into a service they can trust.

## What to watch next

Three sets of verifiable information would help assess this collaboration. First, which specific vehicle models, cities and stations actually offer the service? Second, how are total journey time, waiting and human intervention measured? Third, can users check prices, reservation rules, compatibility restrictions and fault-handling procedures in advance? Models, locations, scale, pricing and performance still need verifiable subsequent disclosures. A direction for cooperation is not a launch announcement.

The value of smart swapping should be tested in everyday use: fewer detours, less waiting and advance knowledge of whether replenishment can be completed. An agreement sets a direction. Consistent, transparent and repeatable operations will provide more substantial evidence in the next stage.
