Reuters, October 19: S&P flags possible extra AI-linked hyperscaler liabilities, unquantified, with no ratings changes currently needed.[^1]

The report raises a specific question: which contracts should be followed to identify the ultimate payer when assessing the credit risk of AI construction? The analysis below draws on public material and the disclosure framework. It does not estimate any company's undisclosed liabilities.

## Separate spending, debt and commitments

Capital expenditure describes how money is used; debt describes a financing obligation. More construction does not mean the entire additional amount is borrowed. Conversely, corporate bond balances alone cannot explain future cash requirements. Loans, leases, purchase commitments and guarantees need to be examined separately. For each, the relevant entity, maturity, trigger conditions and payment priority must be established before assessing which obligations could arrive together.

US rules require discussion of actual or reasonably likely material commitments and contingent obligations involving unconsolidated entities, even without consolidated balance-sheet recognition.[^2] Consequently, absence from the main balance sheet, absence of public disclosure and the fact that payment has not yet been triggered are three distinct findings. One cannot substitute for another.

Double counting is easier to overlook. A project's loan and a guarantee supporting that loan describe the responsibilities of the borrower and guarantor respectively. Adding them together without defining what is being measured can overstate total financing. Yet when assessing one company's exposure, a guarantee cannot be dismissed merely because the project debt belongs to another entity. Aggregate financing and each party's risk exposure are separate questions.

## Contracts can lock in revenue and pressure

The useful starting point is the payment chain, rather than a list of company names. Who buys the computing capacity, pays the site rent, repays the construction loan, or provides support if specified conditions arise? The task is to trace dependencies between those payments. A partnership or an investment alone does not establish that one company must rescue another.

S&P Market Intelligence notes shared hyperscaler exposure and potentially lagging private-credit valuations.[^3] Another study identifies stronger links among AI issuers that may weaken diversification.[^4]

The resulting question is whether revenues supporting different loans repeatedly depend on the same underlying demand. Customer concentration should be separated from recourse under a guarantee. The first can expose businesses to pressure at the same time; the second determines whom a creditor can pursue for payment. The risks can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Even two projects serving the same customer need not suffer the same losses if contract terms, collateral, payment priority and cancellation provisions differ.

## Bond prices and ratings answer different questions

SEC guidance separates repayment risk from market and liquidity risk.[^5] Thus falling prices need not mean default, while unchanged ratings do not guarantee investment value. Both can coexist.

The substantive test is whether cash flow can meet payments as they fall due. Construction spending, contractual receipts and debt maturities do not automatically coincide. A project that may be profitable over its lifetime can still need liquidity support along the way. Conversely, a period of cash-flow pressure does not by itself establish eventual insolvency. An assessment needs to address maturities, available funds and contractual constraints, rather than letting one profit measure stand in for a complete repayment analysis.

## What evidence is needed next?

Future disclosures can be read through three sets of questions. First, when do payments bunch together, which commitments can be adjusted, and what conditions turn contingent responsibilities into actual payments? Second, can continuing usage support revenue, how concentrated are customers, and can the associated receipts be sustained? Third, when pressure arises, where is accessible cash, which funds are restricted, and can additional financing still be obtained?

These are questions for investigation, not findings that companies are already in financial difficulty. Before contracts and payment arrangements have been assembled, presenting uncertainty as a single total of “hidden debt” does not improve transparency. The more useful advance is to identify who bears each obligation, under what conditions and on what timetable, and then keep testing whether cash flow can fulfil those commitments.
