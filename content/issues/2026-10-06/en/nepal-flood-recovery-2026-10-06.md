*Updated October 15, 2026.*

Nepal decided on October 5 to shift the response to the August 26 Bhotekoshi–Trishuli floods toward rehabilitation and reconstruction, ending non-essential active searches while retaining emergency operations. The change marks a new phase of disaster management. It does not mean affected communities are back to normal, or that the question of missing people has been resolved.[^1][^2] The Executive Committee had initiated this shift on September 30, retaining essential searches.[^5]

## Recovery extends beyond the search phase

Reuters reported on October 5, citing Nepalese officials, a death tally of 1,455 (including human remains)[^6], 5,285 people missing and 13,795 people rescued. The head of the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority said remains and property encountered during rehabilitation would still be recovered, with emergency rescues continuing when needed. These were the figures reported at that time.[^1] Missing records may include unidentified remains, rescued people and duplicates.[^6]

The disaster occurred on August 26, not in early October. Nepal’s Foreign Ministry had already documented on September 8 that communities along the river in Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading and other districts had suffered damage to homes, roads, bridges and hydropower infrastructure. Tunnel rescues, identification efforts and the restoration of connections were still being coordinated then.[^3] The river-basin disaster, the monsoon rainfall that followed and the new recovery decision are separate events.

## Estimated needs are not money already available

According to local outlet Onlinekhabar, the National Council for Disaster Risk Reduction and Management recommended on October 5 that the Finance Ministry approve NPR 559.17 billion for 504 rehabilitation and reconstruction plans submitted by ministries and agencies. Further budget recommendations would follow for projects without completed cost estimates or established priorities.[^2] The official notice specifies NPR 559,177,227,000 for Finance Ministry approval.[^7]

Reuters also reported that authorities had previously estimated early recovery and rehabilitation needs at nearly US$5 billion.[^1] The currencies, the plans covered and the estimation stages differ. The two figures should not be treated as the same pot of secured funding. Progress will depend on which projects begin first, when money is disbursed, and how housing, transport, electricity and public services are coordinated.

Restored roads and bridges can bring construction equipment and relief supplies into affected areas. Electricity and telecommunications affect whether residents can return and whether businesses can operate. Reconstruction depends on reconnecting those systems. It is not a matter of simply adding up completed projects.

## Hydropower projects need individual reassessment

Discussions about energy reconstruction were already underway. On September 30, Onlinekhabar cited the Energy Ministry as saying the August 26 floods had damaged 14 hydropower projects with a combined capacity of around 783 MW. Detailed post-disaster needs assessments and project-specific studies were still being developed. This count concerns damaged projects. It is not interchangeable with reports counting destroyed projects, and it does not establish that all 783 MW had been operating or were permanently lost.[^4] The earlier RDNA’s 783.385 MW combines hydro and solar, including construction-stage capacity.[^8]

The report said authorities had not decided whether every project would be rebuilt in its original form. Regulators were studying technical safety standards, disaster warnings and risk-based insurance. Extensions of loan terms or operating periods and financing concessions were also under discussion, rather than adopted as support policies.[^4]

Stronger designs can increase initial costs. Rebuilding exactly as before may recreate exposure to similar risks. A new project appraisal needs to consider lost operating revenue, reconstruction spending, insurance compensation and future income together, not only how quickly the previous capacity figure can be restored. For infrastructure investment exposed to extreme disasters, that is a long-term challenge.

## Families need more than rebuilt homes

Officials also told Reuters that the government was examining how to legally declare missing people dead so relatives could address insurance, banking and other formalities. Those procedures were still being developed.[^1] For many families, legal recognition is tied to inheritance, compensation and everyday arrangements. Starting construction cannot, on its own, remove those barriers.

Recovery has to be followed on two tracks: physical works such as roads, electricity and housing, and institutional support for families of the missing, displaced residents and people whose livelihoods have been damaged. Progress on both is needed if reconstruction funding is to become a lasting recovery.
