## Montenegro reaches 23 provisionally closed EU chapters under the principle that nothing is final until all 33 chapters conclude

The Council of the European Union and the European Commission Directorate-General for Enlargement and Eastern Neighbourhood (DG ENEST) announced that the **30th** EU–Montenegro Accession Conference in Brussels provisionally closed negotiating Chapter **1** (free movement of goods) and Chapter **12** (food safety, veterinary and phytosanitary policy).[^1][^2] On the same day, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) published its flagship assessment in *WMO Bulletin* Vol. 75 (2), documenting that Earth’s Energy Imbalance (EEI) has averaged roughly **0.9 W/m²** over the past two decades and warning of a potential gap in space-based radiation records after **2032**,[^3] while the UC Berkeley College of Chemistry announced that Professor John F. Hartwig received the 2026 Eni Award for Advanced Environmental Solutions for catalytic deconstruction of polyethylene and polypropylene plastics.[^4]

Chaired by Irish Minister of State for European Affairs and Defence Thomas Byrne on behalf of the Council presidency alongside Montenegrin Prime Minister Milojko Spajić, the Brussels session followed the October 12, 2026 closure of Chapters 17, 18 and 19, bringing Montenegro’s tally to **23** provisionally closed chapters out of **33** total chapters opened.[^1] While Byrne noted the European Commission’s assessment that negotiations could realistically close by year-end if necessary reforms are implemented, the Council communiqué underscored strict legal qualifications: agreements on individual chapters are not final until an overall agreement is reached across all chapters, the EU retains the right to reopen provisionally closed chapters if needed, and technical chapter closure still precedes treaty drafting and national parliamentary ratifications.[^1][^2]

## Earth energy imbalance at 0.9 W/m²: deep-ocean monitoring gaps and the post-2032 radiation satellite continuity risk

In global climate monitoring, the *WMO Bulletin* assessment—authored by scientists from Université de Toulouse, NASA JPL, NASA LaRC, NOAA NCEI and Nagoya University—reports that against incoming solar radiation of roughly **340 W/m²** at the top of the atmosphere, Earth’s net energy imbalance has averaged **0.9 W/m²** over the past two decades and has risen significantly since 2004 due to declining planetary reflectivity linked to cloud, surface albedo and aerosol shifts.[^3] More than **90%** of this excess heat is stored as ocean heat content (OHC), **3%–4%** goes into melting sea ice, glaciers and ice sheets, and the combined heat capacity of the atmosphere and land surface equals only the upper **2.5 meters** of the global ocean (less than **1%** of average ocean depth), with up to one quarter of OHC gains since the 1970s occurring below **700 meters**.[^3]

Comparing observational uncertainties, the WMO team notes that top-of-atmosphere satellites track EEI trends within **0.2 W/m²** but cannot calibrate the absolute magnitude alone; the international Argo float array across the upper **2,000 meters** reduced decadal uncertainty to roughly **0.3 W/m²** by 2005, while satellite altimetry-gravimetry geodetic and hybrid methods achieved **0.45 W/m²** and **0.2 W/m²** uncertainties over 2005–2023, leaving the nearly half of ocean volume below 2,000 meters dependent on expanding Deep Argo floats reaching **6,000 meters**.[^3] Crucially, beyond NASA’s Libera mission—projected to end around **2032**—no space agency has yet confirmed a follow-on broadband radiation-budget satellite mission, underscoring that both industrial decarbonization advances such as Hartwig’s catalytic monomer recycling and uninterrupted global observing infrastructure are required in tandem.[^3][^4]
