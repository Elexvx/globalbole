## Institutional landlord fee enforcement: from bait-and-switch advertised rents to security deposit deductions

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) along with the attorneys general of Arizona, Colorado and Indiana and the Utah Division of Consumer Protection filed a federal complaint and stipulated permanent injunction order on November 6, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia (`Civil Action No. 1:26-cv-06782-VMC`, FTC Matter `2423073`), announcing that the nation’s largest single-family rental property manager, Progress Residential, LLC, and its private-equity parent, Pretium Partners, LLC, will pay **$49.75 million** to settle charges of deceptive rental pricing and unlawful deposit practices.[^1] On the same day, the Federal Reserve Board released its G.19 Consumer Credit statistical report for September 2026, showing that total US consumer credit rose at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of **4.2%** in the third quarter and accelerated from a revised **1.9%** annual pace in August to **6.4%** in September.[^2]

According to the FTC and four-state complaint summary, Progress Residential and Pretium Partners misled prospective and current tenants across multiple stages of the single-family rental (SFR) lifecycle: advertising deceptive base rental prices that omitted mandatory monthly add-on fees, failing to inspect homes before residents moved in, providing inadequate maintenance and customer service, improperly deducting charges from tenants’ security deposits upon move-out, and restricting negative consumer reviews.[^1] While the stipulated permanent injunction and $49.75 million monetary judgment—subject to formal federal court entry—establish strict upfront all-in rent disclosure and deposit substantiation requirements, the enforcement action addresses fee deception rather than structural housing supply shortages.[^1]

## Federal Reserve G.19 report: September revolving credit rebound amid 22.36% interest-bearing credit card rates

Alongside rental cost pressures, the Federal Reserve’s September 2026 G.19 release showed total seasonally adjusted consumer credit outstanding reaching **$5,224.4 billion** at the end of September 2026 (preliminary), comprising **$1,368.5 billion** in revolving credit (primarily credit cards) and **$3,856.0 billion** in nonrevolving credit (primarily auto and student loans).[^2] During the third quarter of 2026, total consumer credit expanded at a **4.2%** annual rate (**$214.6 billion** annualized flow), with revolving credit up **4.1%** and nonrevolving credit up **4.2%**; in September alone, revolving credit rebounded from a **-4.4%** annualized contraction (-$59.6 billion) in August to a **14.5%** annualized expansion (**$195.6 billion** annualized flow), while nonrevolving credit rose at a **3.6%** annual rate (**$138.5 billion** annualized flow).[^2]

Two methodological boundaries and interest-rate benchmarks in the G.19 tables frame these household borrowing numbers. First, the G.19 series excludes loans secured by real estate (mortgages); in the unadjusted quarter-end memo items, total student loans stood at **$1,884.6 billion** and motor vehicle loans stood at **$1,589.8 billion** at the end of Q3 2026.[^2] Second, commercial bank borrowing costs remained elevated in the third quarter of 2026: the average stated annual percentage rate (APR) across all commercial bank credit card accounts reached **21.19%**, while the average rate on credit card accounts actually assessed interest stood at **22.36%**, alongside **7.54%** for 60-month new car loans and **11.90%** for 24-month personal loans.[^2] A 14.5% annualized jump in September revolving balances against a 22.36% interest-bearing APR points to both seasonal autumn spending and growing reliance on high-cost revolving credit among liquidity-constrained households.[^2]
