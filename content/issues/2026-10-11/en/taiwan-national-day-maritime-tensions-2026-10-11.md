In his October 10 National Day address outside the Presidential Office Building in Taipei, Lai Ching-te connected greater defence investment, continuity of essential services and dialogue on equal terms. Taiwan would pursue peace while protecting freedom and its ability to choose its future, he said. Stronger self-defence was intended to deter conflict and make dignified engagement possible.[^1]

## Defence extends beyond equipment

Lai outlined continuing defence reform, stronger asymmetric capabilities and indigenous defence industries, drawing on semiconductor, information and communications technology, and precision-manufacturing expertise. He also described continuity in energy, communications, transport, finance, food and healthcare as practical components of whole-of-society resilience, requiring preparation across central and local government and civil society.[^1]

That places security beyond procurement alone. Military preparation cannot by itself sustain social stability if essential services fail; resilient civilian systems, conversely, do not eliminate military threats. Both are needed, but their effectiveness must be assessed separately. Useful tests include whether exercises identify and close gaps, whether agencies can coordinate, and whether basic services continue when communications or transport are disrupted.

The address endorsed increased defence investment without announcing a new spending amount or percentage in the relevant passages. It therefore reiterates a policy direction rather than establishing that a new budget has passed. Funding, implementation and usable capability remain different questions.[^1]

## The conditions for dialogue

Lai welcomed US–China dialogue to manage competition and reduce conflict risks, and urged Indo-Pacific democracies to help preserve regional peace. He also stressed the Taiwanese people’s right to determine their future and the need for engagement based on equality and dignity.[^1]

China’s Taiwan Affairs Office criticised Lai on October 10, reiterating its claim to Taiwan.[^2]

Willingness to talk does not automatically mean accepting the other side’s political premises. Emphasising defence does not, by itself, exclude engagement either. The practical question is whether parties can establish measures to reduce miscalculation while those underlying differences remain unresolved.

## Separate observations from intent

Reuters reported US criticism of Chinese maritime activity east of Taiwan; Taiwanese officials suspected blockade rehearsal.[^3]

Risk assessment should go beyond vessel counts. Whether ports remain operational, supplies can arrive, routes face material restrictions and communication channels remain available says more about the practical consequences than a single image. If those indicators change, assessments should change with the evidence rather than treating the initial interpretation as settled fact.

## What would demonstrate progress?

The address links military preparation, public-service resilience and the conditions for dialogue, but a speech cannot establish that those objectives have been achieved. The next questions concern implementation and whether concrete risk-reduction measures emerge alongside public disagreement. Defence spending signals commitment; service continuity and mechanisms that prevent miscalculation determine how far that commitment produces a more stable environment.
