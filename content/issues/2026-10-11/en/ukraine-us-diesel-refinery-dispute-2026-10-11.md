On October 10, Trump told reporters Ukraine should replace Zelenskyy and stop attacking Russian refineries.[^1]

Whether the fuel trade is legally authorised, what each side wants the other to change, and whether any arrangement actually reduces the harm of the war are not the same inquiry.

## Civilian losses remain central

Ukraine’s emergency service reported 20 dead, including three children, after Russia’s October 10 Zaporizhzhia airstrike. Rescue continued.[^2]

That bulletin records one event, at one time. It is not a number to fold into other dates or other places. Lives, infrastructure and the safety of transport are not background for a forecast of the fuel price.

A diesel announcement and an attack can follow one another without either proving the cause of the other. A diplomatic or economic step can be tested more directly: a clear commitment to stop attacks, evidence that it is being kept, and a response when it is broken. Absent those, more trade does not show that people are safer.

## Britain emphasises sanctions and an energy ceasefire

In its October 10 account of a call with Zelenskyy, Downing Street said Prime Minister Andy Burnham would continue strengthening sanctions and constraining Russia’s war-making capacity, building on new British designations that week. The leaders said Russia should agree to an immediate energy ceasefire, ending attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure and Black Sea shipping. The readout argued this would release more energy and food onto world markets. They also agreed to meet in the coming days.[^3]

The British account ties a larger supply of energy to an end to the destruction, and it keeps the sanctions in place while saying so. Opening the trade in fuel is a different move. One tries to limit damage to the systems that supply energy. The other tries to widen what can be bought and sold. The two might be combined. A licence to trade does not meet the conditions of the energy ceasefire the British account is describing.

## The trading window still has boundaries

OFAC’s General License 135, issued on October 9, authorises specified transactions involving the sale, delivery, offloading and importation of Russian-origin diesel, including US imports, through 12:01 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time on April 7, 2027. It retains a restriction on debiting accounts held at US financial institutions by Russia’s central bank, National Wealth Fund and Ministry of Finance.[^4]

Nothing in the licence is a ceasefire, and nothing in it obliges anyone to stop an attack. A lawful trade, a cargo that actually arrives, and a real easing of the fighting each have to be checked on their own. The document changes what the parties may do commercially. It does not show that diesel has landed, and it does not stand in for oversight of a security commitment.

The argument is larger than the import window. If one side treats the trade as a way to cut costs, and another treats the plants as instruments of wartime pressure, someone still has to say who pays, whether the demands run both ways, and how compliance is checked. A joint statement in favour of peace does not answer that.

## Watch for arrangements that can be tested

What would move the argument is a text with terms in it: the facilities and the transport an energy ceasefire covers, the party that verifies compliance, the order in which commitments and sanctions changes take effect, and whether attacks on civilian infrastructure actually fall. For civilians, protection that can be checked matters sooner than any side’s claim that it is ready to talk.
