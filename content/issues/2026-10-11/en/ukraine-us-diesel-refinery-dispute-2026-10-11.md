On October 10, Trump told reporters Ukraine should replace Zelenskyy and stop attacking Russian refineries.[^1]

Three questions require separate scrutiny: whether fuel transactions are legally authorised, what each party wants the other to change, and whether an arrangement actually reduces the harm of war.

## Civilian losses remain central

Ukraine’s emergency service reported 20 dead, including three children, after Russia’s October 10 Zaporizhzhia airstrike; rescue continued.[^2]

A casualty bulletin is a time-specific record of a particular event, not a figure to merge casually with other dates or locations. It also underlines why lives, infrastructure and transport safety cannot be treated merely as background to a fuel-price forecast.

The sequence of a diesel policy announcement and an attack cannot, on its own, establish that one caused the other. A more testable question is whether a diplomatic or economic measure produces a clear commitment to stop attacks, evidence of compliance, and a response to violations. Without those elements, increased commerce alone cannot demonstrate an improvement in security.

## Britain emphasises sanctions and an energy ceasefire

In its October 10 account of a call with Zelenskyy, Downing Street said Prime Minister Andy Burnham would continue strengthening sanctions and constraining Russia’s war-making capacity, building on new British designations that week. The leaders said Russia should agree to an immediate energy ceasefire, ending attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure and Black Sea shipping. The readout argued this would release more energy and food onto world markets. They also agreed to meet in the coming days.[^3]

The British account links better supply to ending destruction while maintaining sanctions pressure. That differs from simply widening access to tradable fuel: one approach seeks to reduce damage to supply systems, the other to expand trading opportunities. They might be combined, but a trade authorisation cannot be assumed to satisfy the other policy’s requirements.

## The trading window still has boundaries

OFAC’s General License 135, issued on October 9, authorises specified transactions involving the sale, delivery, offloading and importation of Russian-origin diesel, including US imports, through 12:01 a.m. Eastern Daylight Time on April 7, 2027. It retains a restriction on debiting accounts held at US financial institutions by Russia’s central bank, National Wealth Fund and Ministry of Finance.[^4]

The document is not a ceasefire agreement and sets out no obligation to stop attacks. Lawful trading, commercial delivery and de-escalation therefore require separate verification. A licence changes the parties’ commercial options without establishing that cargoes have arrived or replacing oversight of security commitments.

That is what takes the latest dispute beyond a question about diesel imports. If one party treats energy trade as a cost-reduction tool while another regards the relevant facilities as part of wartime pressure, coordination must address who bears the costs, whether demands are reciprocal and how implementation is checked. A shared declaration in favour of peace does not resolve those questions.

## Watch for arrangements that can be tested

The critical issue is whether concrete provisions emerge: which facilities and transport activities an energy ceasefire covers, who verifies compliance, how commitments and sanctions changes are sequenced, and whether attacks on civilian infrastructure decrease. For civilians, verifiable protection matters more immediately than any party’s assertions about willingness to negotiate.
