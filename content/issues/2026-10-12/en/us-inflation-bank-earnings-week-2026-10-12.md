The United States enters a concentrated week of economic releases and bank results. The Bureau of Labor Statistics schedules September's Consumer Price Index (CPI) for October 14 and its Producer Price Index (PPI) for October 15, both at 8:30 a.m. Eastern time. The Census Bureau's 2026 calendar puts September's advance retail and food-services sales at the same time on October 15. These measure prices paid by consumers, prices received by producers and merchants' sales receipts, respectively. They are not interchangeable indicators.[^1][^2]

JPMorganChase's third-quarter earnings call is scheduled for October 13.[^3]

## August's baseline shows why the composition matters

Before the September release, August CPI provides the starting point: the overall index rose 0.4% month over month after seasonal adjustment and 3.4% year over year without seasonal adjustment. Excluding food and energy, the increases were 0.3% and 2.4%, respectively. Gasoline rose 3.9% during the month, accounting for more than one-third of the overall monthly increase; shelter rose 0.3%. These are August results, not September forecasts.[^4]

That composition means the next report deserves more than a glance at its annual rate. A year-over-year comparison spans 12 months and is also affected by the comparison base a year earlier. The seasonally adjusted monthly change is closer to the latest movement, but one month can still be volatile. An overall increase driven mainly by energy conveys a different pressure pattern from simultaneous acceleration in shelter and other components. Assessing whether pressure is spreading requires both component contributions and several months of observations.[^4][^5]

Core CPI excludes relatively volatile food and energy prices, helping readers examine other price trends. It does not remove households' food or fuel bills. CPI also represents an average consumption basket, rather than every household: different shares of transport, housing or medical spending can produce different personal inflation experiences. Slower inflation likewise does not mean a lower price level; it means prices are rising more slowly over the relevant comparison period.[^5]

## PPI adds the producer perspective, without mechanically predicting CPI

August's final-demand PPI increased 0.4% month over month after seasonal adjustment and 5.4% year over year without adjustment. Final-demand goods rose 1.1% during the month, while services rose 0.1%. Energy goods increased 4.2%, and diesel fuel rose 24.1%. Producer-price increases were therefore unevenly distributed, making separate examination of goods, energy and services important.[^6]

PPI records prices received by domestic producers when selling goods and services; CPI focuses on prices consumers pay. Taxes, subsidies, distribution costs and differences in coverage can lead the indexes in different directions. A PPI increase cannot simply be transferred to CPI, nor can the gap between the two be used on its own to calculate business profits.[^7]

A more useful comparison this week is whether the new producer-side energy and service figures corroborate evidence in consumer-price components. If they diverge, the first question is what is being measured and at which transaction stage, rather than whether one statistic is defective. Using this comparison to assess future pressure remains a conditional analysis, not a confirmed finding of price transmission.[^5][^7]

## Rising retail receipts still require a price-volume distinction

The Census Bureau adjusts monthly retail-sales series for seasonal, holiday and trading-day differences, but not for price changes. Higher sales receipts may reflect more purchases, higher unit prices or a different mix of purchases. An increase in spending at the pump therefore needs to be considered alongside fuel prices; it does not automatically mean consumers bought more gasoline.[^8]

Coverage also matters. Retail figures are based on establishments primarily engaged in retail trade and grouped by store type. They do not measure all sales of a particular product across every channel. Even with food services included, they do not cover all household services consumption. Simply deflating retail growth with headline CPI can therefore introduce a mismatch between the consumer basket and the composition of retail sales. Comparing the relevant categories and prices first is a sounder basis for interpreting sales growth.[^9][^5]

## Bank results offer another layer of evidence

Bank accounts can add information beyond price and spending statistics, but industry benchmarks must remain separate from individual large-bank results. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's second-quarter report, released August 25, covered 4,238 insured institutions. Combined net income was $90.1 billion, up 12.0% from the previous quarter; return on assets was 1.37%, and net interest margin was 3.32%. Those figures describe the second quarter, not the third-quarter results about to arrive.[^10]

Domestic deposits increased 0.8% during that quarter and loans rose 1.8%, while past-due and nonaccrual measures and the net charge-off rate improved from the previous quarter. In the next earnings round, useful comparisons include whether revenue growth accompanies changes in deposits and lending, and whether asset quality holds up. One company's performance cannot stand for the entire industry.[^10]

Interest-rate changes do not benefit all banks equally. Regulators' explanation of interest-rate risk emphasizes that asset and liability repricing dates, maturity structures and rate sensitivities affect net interest income and economic asset values. Funding costs can change when loan yields rise. The direction of market rates alone therefore cannot establish that bank profits must rise or fall.[^11]

Together, this week's releases address three distinct questions: where price pressure is concentrated, how much changes in sales receipts reflect actual purchasing changes, and whether financial institutions' income and credit conditions corroborate each other. Before publication, those remain questions to test. Separating actual results, historical comparisons and conditional inferences prevents one strong profit figure or price fluctuation from becoming an unsupported verdict on the whole economy.
