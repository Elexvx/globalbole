The United States enters a concentrated week of economic releases and bank results. The Bureau of Labor Statistics schedules September's Consumer Price Index (CPI) for October 14 and its Producer Price Index (PPI) for October 15, both at 8:30 a.m. Eastern time. The Census Bureau's 2026 calendar puts September's advance retail and food-services sales at the same time on October 15. One is the price a consumer pays, one the price a producer receives, and one a merchant's sales receipts. They do not stand in for each other.[^1][^2]

JPMorganChase's third-quarter earnings call is scheduled for October 13.[^3]

## August's baseline shows why the composition matters

Before the September release, August CPI is the starting point. The overall index rose 0.4% month over month after seasonal adjustment and 3.4% year over year without seasonal adjustment. Excluding food and energy, the increases were 0.3% and 2.4%, respectively. Gasoline rose 3.9% during the month, accounting for more than one-third of the overall monthly increase. Shelter rose 0.3%. These are August results, not September forecasts.[^4]

The annual rate, on its own, will not be enough. A comparison with a year earlier covers 12 months, and it is also affected by the comparison base a year before. The seasonally adjusted monthly change is closer to the latest move. One month can still jump around. A rise carried by energy is a different pattern from shelter and the other components accelerating together. Whether the pressure is spreading shows up in the component contributions, and only across several months of observations.[^4][^5]

Core CPI excludes relatively volatile food and energy prices, which helps readers examine other price trends. It does not remove households' food or fuel bills. CPI also represents an average consumption basket, rather than every household. Different shares of transport, housing or medical spending can produce different personal inflation experiences. Slower inflation does not mean a lower price level. It means prices are rising more slowly over the relevant comparison period.[^5]

## PPI adds the producer perspective, without mechanically predicting CPI

August's final-demand PPI increased 0.4% month over month after seasonal adjustment and 5.4% year over year without adjustment. Final-demand goods rose 1.1% during the month, while services rose 0.1%. Energy goods increased 4.2%, and diesel fuel rose 24.1%. Producer-price increases were unevenly distributed, so goods, energy and services need to be examined separately.[^6]

PPI records what domestic producers receive when they sell. CPI records what consumers pay. Taxes, subsidies, distribution costs and differences in what each index covers can send them in different directions. A rise in PPI does not automatically appear in CPI, and the gap between the two is not a profit figure.[^7]

The useful comparison this week is whether the new energy and services numbers on the producer side show up in the consumer-price components. If they do not, ask what is being measured and at which stage of the transaction before concluding that one statistic is wrong. Reading the comparison as a sign of pressure still to come is conditional. It is not a confirmed finding that prices have been passed through.[^5][^7]

## Rising retail receipts still require a price-volume distinction

The Census Bureau adjusts monthly retail-sales series for seasonal, holiday and trading-day differences, but not for price changes. Higher sales receipts may reflect more purchases, higher unit prices or a different mix of purchases. An increase in spending at the pump needs to be considered alongside fuel prices. It does not automatically mean consumers bought more gasoline.[^8]

Coverage is the other trap. The figures come from establishments whose main business is retail, grouped by the type of store. They do not capture every sale of a product, in every channel. Food services are in the series. The rest of the services households buy are not. Deflating the growth with headline CPI can mix a consumer basket with a retail mix that is not the same basket. The cleaner reading starts with the relevant category and the relevant price.[^9][^5]

## Bank results offer another layer of evidence

Bank accounts can add information beyond price and spending statistics, but industry benchmarks must remain separate from individual large-bank results. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation's second-quarter report, released August 25, covered 4,238 insured institutions. Combined net income was $90.1 billion, up 12.0% from the previous quarter. Return on assets was 1.37%, and net interest margin was 3.32%. Those figures describe the second quarter, not the third-quarter results about to arrive.[^10]

Domestic deposits increased 0.8% during that quarter and loans rose 1.8%, while past-due and nonaccrual measures and the net charge-off rate improved from the previous quarter. In the next earnings round, useful comparisons include whether revenue growth accompanies changes in deposits and lending, and whether asset quality holds up. One company's performance cannot stand for the entire industry.[^10]

A move in interest rates does not land evenly. Regulators' account of interest-rate risk emphasizes that repricing dates, maturity structures and rate sensitivities, on both assets and liabilities, feed into net interest income and into the economic value of assets. Funding costs can rise even as loan yields rise. The direction of market rates, taken alone, does not say that bank profits must rise or must fall.[^11]

Until the figures are published, a strong profit or a sharp move in a price is not a verdict on the economy. The CPI release will show where the price pressure sits. The retail figures will show receipts, which still have to be split into prices and volumes. Bank results will show income and credit, and whether the two move together. August, and the second quarter, are benchmarks already in hand. They are not the September answers, and they are not the third-quarter answers. Published results, comparisons with the past, and inferences that are still conditional have to be kept apart.
