## Operating permission and the community subscription model

PowerBank announced on November 3 that its ground-mounted Camillus community solar project in New York had received permission to operate. The company described this as an important step toward bringing the project into service. The installation occupies a closed landfill and has a previously disclosed design capacity of **3.26 MW on the direct-current side**. Permission to operate authorises it to begin running; it does **not establish how much electricity has entered the grid**, and design capacity alone cannot determine this year's generation. The announcement provided no measured output, connected-subscriber count or total electricity-bill credits already delivered.[^1][^2]

Community solar allows renters and homeowners who do not install panels on their own roofs to subscribe to a share of a project and receive electricity-bill credits under rules linked to its generation. Its practical value lies in broadening access, rather than guaranteeing an identical saving for every subscriber. The eventual credit still depends on actual generation, subscription arrangements, local billing rules and ongoing project operations. PowerBank said Solar Simplified handles customer acquisition, enrollment and subscription services. Solar Advocate Development LLC owns the project.[^1]

## Earlier transaction value is distinct from realised benefits

The **US$7.3 million** highlighted in the announcement's title is not financing newly obtained on November 3. PowerBank's predecessor, SolarBank, announced on **March 19, 2025**, that the project had been sold and would be built for the buyer, with the sale and engineering, procurement and construction arrangements valued at approximately US$7.3 million. That earlier announcement also explained that receipt of the full contract value was subject to construction, financing and agreement-performance risks. The new milestone is permission to operate, rather than a newly signed transaction or recognition of all revenue.[^2]

Developing a closed landfill can give constrained land a new energy use, but it does not automatically remove uncertainty about site maintenance, equipment life or subscriber economics. The conclusion supported at present is that a specific project has cleared an operating-permission milestone, allowing its community subscription model to begin operating. Assessing the environmental and household benefits will require verifiable generation, operating and electricity-bill data.
