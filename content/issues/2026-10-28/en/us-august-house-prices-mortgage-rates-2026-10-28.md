The US Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) reported on October 27 that its seasonally adjusted national house-price index rose 0.3% in August from the previous month and 2.4% from a year earlier. July's monthly increase was revised from 0.3% to 0.2%.[^1] An October publication date does not mean these figures describe October house prices.

## Modest national growth does not mean every region is moving together

Across the nine census divisions, August's monthly changes ranged from a 0.8% decline in New England to a 2.1% increase in the East South Central division.[^1] That spread shows why a national index cannot be applied directly to every city, let alone used to price a particular home for sale. The national trend and conditions in a specific location are different levels of analysis.

Monthly and annual comparisons also answer different questions. A monthly change compares adjacent months; an annual change looks back one year. Positive readings for both show that this index increased over both comparison periods, but do not establish that future months will grow at the same pace. A single month's movement, the continuation of a trend and a forecast should be discussed separately.

Revisions matter too. Comparisons between consecutive months should use the latest consistent data release, not combine an older July figure with a newer August figure. Otherwise, seemingly small differences between versions can alter the judgment about whether price growth accelerated.

## An index measures change, rather than valuing every home

FHFA's flagship index uses purchase transactions associated with Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, applying a weighted repeat-sales method to compare sale prices for the same property at different times.[^2] Its focus is measuring price change, rather than simply averaging the prices of homes sold during a month.

The distinction has practical importance. If the types or price ranges of homes sold change in a particular month, the average transaction price may also change even if individual properties have not moved in value to the same extent. The repeat-sales method seeks to distinguish these changes in transaction mix from price changes, but remains constrained by its sample coverage.

The index is therefore neither a complete record of every residential transaction nor a real-time measure of asking prices. It cannot fully represent cash purchases, different loan types or individual property conditions. For households, a national index supplies context but cannot replace local comparable sales, the condition of a home and their own financing arrangements.

## Interest rates follow a different timeline

Freddie Mac's average 30-year fixed mortgage rate for the week of October 22 was 7.32%, compared with 7.40% a week earlier and 6.19% a year earlier.[^3] This is a different statistical reference point, not a rate that can be backdated to the borrowing environment behind August's house-price movements. The survey uses loan-application data to construct a market indicator, not a personalized quote available to every borrower.[^4]

Both prices and interest rates affect affordability, but through different mechanisms. Prices influence the amount needed to buy a home; financing terms influence the cost of borrowed funds. Down payments, loan terms and household income also change what a buyer can afford. Even modest national price growth does not necessarily make housing feel more affordable. Conversely, a weekly rate decline is not enough to establish that every buyer's monthly payment will fall in step.

These relationships help explain the market, but two sets of figures from different dates cannot establish causation on their own. Assessing how rates affect transactions and prices requires transaction, demand and loan data for the relevant periods, rather than treating two indicators moving in different directions as proof that one caused the other.

## Assessing the market requires preserving both scale and timing

The release establishes that, through August, the national prices measured by FHFA were still rising modestly, with regional differences. It does not provide October prices for every city or a comprehensive assessment of households' current affordability.

Subsequent analysis should continue to distinguish prices, transaction volumes and financing conditions. Rising prices can coexist with subdued trading, while national trends can differ from developments in individual regions. Keeping each indicator within its own sample, reference period and geography avoids mistaking a useful statistical tool for a real-time answer about the entire housing market.
