The US Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued General License 135 on October 9, temporarily authorising transactions involving Russian-origin diesel. The measure expands the scope for trading a particular fuel. It does not dismantle the sanctions system. What the licence authorises, what it still restricts, and whether commercial deliveries materialise are separate questions.[^2]

## From discussions to workable arrangements

Reuters’ source said US, Ukrainian, European and NATO representatives held six-hour talks in Miami on October 9, seeking a common proposal for Russia.[^1]

Hromadske Radio, citing RFE/RL reporter Alex Raufoglu’s sources, reports an early finish and no second day.[^5]

Any diplomatic arrangement to end a war has to move through distinct stages. The parties need language they can discuss together, then a determination of what the relevant parties accept, then mechanisms for implementation and monitoring. Progress at one stage does not complete the next. Where long-term security and economic recovery are concerned, who carries each obligation, when conditions are triggered and how violations are addressed often say more about feasibility than the duration of the talks.

## The diesel licence has explicit legal limits

OFAC authorises transactions otherwise prohibited by the Russian Harmful Foreign Activities Sanctions Regulations or the Ukraine-/Russia-Related Sanctions Regulations that relate to the sale, delivery, offloading or importation of Russian-origin diesel, explicitly including imports into the United States. The authorisation runs through 12:01 a.m. US Eastern Daylight Time on April 7, 2027. It does not allow debits to accounts held at US financial institutions by Russia’s central bank, National Wealth Fund or Ministry of Finance.[^2]

The legal space for trade in this particular fuel has changed. Not every Russian energy transaction is permitted, and the sanctions system has not been abolished. Authorising a transaction is separate from producing, loading and delivering the fuel. The licence alone establishes neither that promised supply volumes have materialised nor that every proposed payment arrangement complies with applicable rules.

## Fuel costs and sanctions pressure pull in different directions

Mediaite reports Trump sought cheaper fuel, and that Zelenskyy opposed sanctions relief without de-escalation.[^3]

There was public opposition within the United States as well. Senators Jeanne Shaheen, Chuck Schumer and Elizabeth Warren issued a joint statement on October 9 arguing that the diesel measure undermined support for Ukraine and European allies, as well as efforts to restrict Russia’s war revenue.[^4]

The two policy goals do not sit easily together. Expanding the supplies that can be traded may widen buyers’ options. Relaxing restrictions may also increase sellers’ revenue. The eventual price effect depends on supply that is genuinely additional, on whether existing trade is simply rerouted, and on transport and delivery that succeed. A presidential announcement, or a licence alone, does not establish that diesel prices will fall substantially.

## Watch the text and the deliveries

The diplomacy turns on how the relevant parties’ commitments fit together. The diesel measure turns on whether permitted transactions become actual supplies. A commercial arrangement does not settle long-term security questions, and talks that continue do not mean the disagreements have disappeared. Legal authorisation, commercial execution and political commitments require separate tests.

Reactions on announcement day are a thin basis for judging the policy. Transaction and delivery records, and commitments clear enough to execute, carry more weight. Fuel supply and diplomacy may follow different timetables. Only the sequence of implementation and verifiable results can establish whether a temporary authorisation meets its objectives.
