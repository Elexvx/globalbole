## The business begins before a processing plant

Waste infrastructure connects collection, transport, sorting, treatment and final outlets. A truck journey is only one stage. A useful way to assess this kind of business is to ask whether each stage delivers material that the next stage can accept, at an economically sustainable cost. The following industry analysis uses Australia's National waste and resource recovery report 2024, published on 20 January 2025, chiefly covering 2022–23. These are historical national figures, not Cleanaway operating results or measurements of the market in October 2026.[^2]

The report records approximately 76 million tonnes of waste in its headline scope, which includes ash. Municipal solid waste accounted for 13.5 million tonnes, commercial and industrial sources including ash for 32.9 million, and construction and demolition for 29.2 million. The narrower core-waste measure was about 65.4 million tonnes. Rounded figures should not be treated as exact sums.[^2]

This mix matters commercially. Household bins, demolition materials and industrial waste do not require identical routes, equipment or processing. A national tonnage total cannot establish a company's addressable market. To estimate that, an investor would also need information on geography, permissions, contracts, competing facilities and the types of material each site can receive.

## Route density and downstream capacity must match

Local governments collected roughly 9.9 million tonnes through kerbside bins in 2022–23: 6.02 million tonnes of garbage, 1.80 million of dry recyclables and 2.07 million of organics. The report also describes local authorities as important owners and operators of transfer stations, recovery facilities and landfills, especially in rural areas.[^2]

The economic implication is that private infrastructure operates within a wider service network. More customers along an existing route can spread vehicle and labour costs over more collections. But extra collection volume does not automatically improve profitability if it also requires long journeys to a suitable treatment site. Transfer capacity, plant availability and the destination for remaining waste all affect the cost of a service.

Likewise, a larger plant needs dependable feedstock. Building nominal capacity without sufficient suitable material can leave expensive equipment underused; taking more material than downstream systems can manage can create storage and disposal costs. These are analytical considerations for any waste network, not findings about a specific Cleanaway facility.

## A recycling bin is an input, not a finished product

The national report estimates that materials recovery facilities received about 2 million tonnes in 2022–23 and sent around 21% of receipts to landfill. Rejection can reflect contamination, unsuitable presentation, very small items or damage during sorting. It describes a combination of manual and automated equipment, including magnets, optical sorting and air separation.[^2]

This creates two different measures of performance: tonnes received and usable material produced. Increasing the first does not guarantee an equal increase in the second. An investment case should therefore test output quality, reject rates, downtime and the cost of disposing of residues alongside headline throughput.

There is also a boundary problem in measurement. The report explains that recycling may be recorded at different stages and that stockpiles or rejected material can distort comparisons. Material is not fully recycled until it is used in new products, buildings or infrastructure. For buyers of a waste business, consistent measurement is essential: otherwise a change in reporting can resemble an operational improvement.

## Recovery is a market as well as a process

Sorted material still needs a customer. Different outputs have different values and quality requirements; the report contrasts higher-value aluminium with lower-value mixed paper. Its historical price discussion should not be read as a quotation of current commodity prices.[^2]

A useful analytical distinction is between income for receiving or collecting waste and the value obtainable from recovered products. Processing can produce a technically recyclable material whose sale proceeds are insufficient to cover sorting and transport. Conversely, cleaner source-separated material can reduce later processing costs. Who carries fluctuations in commodity values depends on the contracts, which must be examined rather than assumed.

Organic waste illustrates how a new collection service also needs an end market. The report found that about 53% of Australians had access to a kerbside organics service in 2022–23, and about 28% to combined food-and-garden collection. Collected material was generally composted for urban or agricultural use.[^2] Expanding collection therefore entails more than distributing bins: contamination control, processing capacity and reliable demand for the resulting product have to develop together.

## Higher recovery does not describe every environmental outcome

The historical national resource-recovery rate was 66%, while recycling was about 63%. Energy recovery accounted for much of the difference and was mostly associated with landfill-gas electricity generation. Construction and demolition recovery was approximately 81%, compared with about 52% for municipal solid waste.[^2]

Those differences caution against treating all tonnes as interchangeable. A portfolio weighted toward heavy construction materials can show a different aggregate recovery rate from one handling mixed household waste, even before considering operational quality. Recovery rates also do not directly measure emissions avoided, product durability or waste prevented. Those outcomes need their own evidence.

For public purchasers, the practical questions are consequently specific: service reliability, the quality and destination of recovered material, residual disposal and transparent reporting. For an investor, the same questions translate into operating costs, investment needs and the durability of revenue. A circular-economy label does not remove the need to examine them.

## Separate transaction certainty from operating value

Enterprise value and the amount ultimately received by shareholders are different financial concepts. Comparing a business-wide valuation directly with an equity cheque would mix measures. Separately, signing a transaction does not by itself transfer ownership: the remaining approval process is a distinct source of uncertainty.

The operational test will remain the same whoever owns the assets: whether collection routes, processing facilities and material buyers form a reliable chain. Evidence worth following includes usable output rather than intake alone, maintenance and investment requirements, and the ability to sustain service while changing technology. This is an analytical checklist, not a forecast of approval, returns or future company policy.
