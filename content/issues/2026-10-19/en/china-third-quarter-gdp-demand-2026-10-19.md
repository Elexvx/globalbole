China’s economy accelerated in the third quarter, but stronger aggregate growth does not mean all industries experienced the same improvement. On October 19, the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) reported year-on-year GDP growth of 4.5%, up from 4.3% in the second quarter. Growth for the first nine months was 4.6%, with output valued at 107.1071 trillion yuan. These are the NBS’s preliminary estimates. The more important question is which activities are expanding and which sources of demand remain weak.[^1]

The comparison periods matter. The 4.5% figure compares the third quarter with the same period in 2025. Seasonally adjusted quarter-on-quarter growth was 1.2%, against a revised 0.9% in the second quarter. These measures must neither be added together nor confused with an annualized rate. GDP’s monetary level uses current prices; its growth rate removes price effects. It does not imply that sales, wages and profits all rose by 4.5%.[^1][^2]

## Why stronger production does not automatically mean stronger consumption

Production provided visible support. Industrial value added at enterprises above the designated size rose 5.3% in real terms over the first nine months. Equipment manufacturing grew 10.2% and high-tech manufacturing 14.3%. Services value added increased 5.2%, compared with 3.8% for the secondary sector. The differences suggest that additional output was concentrated in particular manufacturing activities and services. A few fast-growing industries cannot stand in for the entire business sector.[^1]

Retail sales of consumer goods, meanwhile, rose just 1.1% in nominal terms over the first nine months. Excluding vehicles, growth was 2.8%, indicating that vehicle-related sales weighed on the overall measure. Online goods sales increased 3.8% and catering revenue 2.4%, showing differences between channels and categories. Retail sales do not cover all services consumption, however, so they do not establish that total household consumption grew only 1.1%. Nor does the gap with real GDP directly measure a loss of purchasing power.[^3]

Economically, the path from additional output to greater household spending runs through orders, business earnings, employment and wages. Equipment and technology upgrades may first improve efficiency, without immediately or evenly raising household cash flow. Stronger production and subdued conditions for some retailers can therefore coexist. This is an explanation of a possible transmission mechanism, not a causal finding established by this statistical release.

## Investment pressures extend beyond property

Investment shows an even sharper divergence. Fixed-asset investment excluding rural households fell 7.2% in the first nine months, and private investment dropped 10.4%. Purchases of equipment, tools and instruments nevertheless rose 9.7%, while investment in intellectual-property products increased 9.3%. A company can cut construction and overall expansion while replacing machinery or investing in technology. Those choices are not contradictory. Manufacturing investment fell 2.1% and infrastructure investment 4.2%, indicating that weak investment cannot be attributed entirely to housing.[^4]

These investment rates do not remove price effects. “On a comparable basis” refers to adjustments such as statistical coverage; it is not equivalent to a constant-price calculation. Comparing them directly with real industrial value-added growth mixes measurement bases. Fixed-asset investment’s decline also cannot be treated as the precise drag from capital formation on GDP, because their coverage and accounting differ.[^4][^5]

Property remains an important constraint on demand. Development investment fell 20.6%, the floor area of newly built properties sold on the market dropped 13.2%, and funds received by developers declined 21.6%. At the same time, the floor area covered by registered contracts for second-hand housing transactions rose 10.7%. More housing transactions and stronger developer finances are different developments: existing-home sales mainly transfer properties between their owners, without directly providing presale cash to developers or automatically starting new construction.[^6]

This distinction explains why transactions alone are insufficient to assess housing’s effect on the economy. If improvement is concentrated in existing homes, brokerage, renovation and moving services may benefit first. Assessing the implications for development investment also requires evidence about new-home sales, financing and construction activity. Those possible subsequent effects are not included as measured growth in this article.

## What to watch in the fourth quarter

Businesses assessing demand need to consider sector-specific orders, prices and payments alongside GDP. For households, income and job security usually bear more directly on spending decisions than aggregate output. Policy effectiveness must also be tested through these links: whether equipment upgrades generate lasting orders, whether new-home sales improve project cash flow, and whether consumption gains spread beyond particular channels to a wider population.

An industry’s growth rate is not its contribution to aggregate growth. Rapid expansion in a small sector may still add relatively little output; scale and value-added weights matter. The reported rates cannot simply be added to determine how many percentage points an industry contributed, much less how much growth a single policy caused.

The figures support a judgment of improving growth alongside persistent divergence. They do not yet establish a broad strengthening of demand. Subsequent releases should show whether retail and private investment improve sustainably, whether developers’ funding pressure eases, and whether production growth feeds more fully into employment and income. Quarterly GDP is preliminary and may be revised as underlying information improves. One stronger quarter cannot settle the outlook for the full year or every industry.[^7]
