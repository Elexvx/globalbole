The conference in Ningde on October 15 emphasized avoiding blind battery expansion and developing storage applications. MIIT’s report announced no approvals or subsidy rules.[^1]

Once the cells are made, the supply-chain question is how they become reliable energy services that earn revenue. Battery manufacturing and the operation of storage projects are different businesses. Manufacturers focus on materials, processes, yields and shipments. Operators also have to decide when to charge and discharge, whom they serve, and how those services are paid for. More manufacturing capacity can improve the supply of equipment. It cannot, by itself, replace the access arrangements and market rules that govern power systems.

## Falling costs widen the scope for applications

The historical numbers come from the International Energy Agency’s April 2024 report, Batteries and Secure Energy Transitions. From 2010 to 2023, lithium-ion battery prices fell from about USD 1,400 per kilowatt-hour to less than USD 140, a decline of roughly 90%. The report ties that fall to research and development and to economies of scale in manufacturing. Lower prices helped batteries move from important components of consumer electronics to large-scale inputs for transport and for electricity systems.[^2]

The same report records 42 gigawatts of battery-storage capacity added in the global power sector in 2023. Deployment more than doubled from the year before. The numbers are a 2023 baseline, and they are historical. Gigawatts measure power. Gigawatt-hours measure the energy stored.

Cheaper batteries matter beyond the price of the cells. Lower equipment expenditure can bring applications that were previously less economic into consideration, including utility-scale installations, storage on the customer side of household and business meters, and small off-grid power systems. Battery prices and the cost of a complete project remain different measures. When the IEA discusses project costs, it explicitly includes installation, other components and development expenses. A buyer who compares options solely through cell prices can miss other spending required to deliver the same service.

## A battery can serve different needs

Storage is easiest to understand as a shift in time. It moves electricity from one period to another and helps address mismatches between generation and consumption. The IEA identifies batteries as well suited to providing short-term flexibility for one to eight continuous hours, and also as able to respond quickly to system signals in support of grid stability. Those are distinct jobs. One is the ability to supply electricity over a stretch of hours. The other is speed and precision.[^2]

A project is not evaluated by counting the batteries on site. The relevant question is the problem it is there to solve: an evening demand peak, local network congestion, or an operational problem that needs rapid adjustment. Planned capacity, actual operation and revenue sources need to line up. Where the service on offer does not match what the local system needs, more equipment may not deliver a corresponding increase in useful value.

The application also shapes the technology. Looking back at the 2023 market, the IEA reports that lithium iron phosphate batteries accounted for around 80% of new battery storage that year. Energy density, it explains, is generally less important in stationary storage than in vehicles, so factors such as cost and lifetime carry more weight. The choice of technology has to follow the intended use, rather than a comparison on a single performance metric.

## Market rules determine whether services generate revenue

Being able to provide a service does not, by itself, get an operator paid. The IEA’s policy recommendations emphasize access to appropriate markets and price signals that recognize batteries’ different contributions to the power system. The report also discusses customer-side batteries. Tariffs that better reflect changes in system costs can help align users’ charging and discharging decisions with grid needs.[^3]

The storage industry does not end at the factory gate. After the equipment is delivered, a project still has to operate inside a particular electricity system and a particular trading environment. Validating what the equipment can do, and validating the assumptions about who pays, deserve comparable weight. One is whether the task can be performed consistently. The other is who pays for it, and under which rules.

Where it is feasible, the IEA also suggests aggregating distributed customer-side batteries into virtual power plants that can provide services similar to larger installations. The hard part is coordinating many smaller resources. Simply adding up their equipment capacities is not the same task. Whether projects can be aggregated, which markets they may enter, and how services are measured and settled, all depend on local rules.

## Long-term demand does not guarantee every expansion succeeds

Battery demand has to be split between historical statistics and scenario calculations. In the Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario in its 2024 report, the IEA puts global battery-storage power capacity at around 1,200 gigawatts in 2030. That describes the changes required along a particular energy and climate pathway. It is not an unconditional forecast of orders.[^2]

Geographic concentration runs along the battery supply chain, the report notes, from mining and materials processing through to cell manufacturing. Technological innovation, recycling and a more diverse supply are the areas it flags for attention. Building more factories is only one part of how the industry develops. Reliability of supply, efficiency in the use of materials, and what happens to products at the end of their useful lives also shape who stays competitive.

For anyone watching the industry, the useful evidence is about how projects actually run. Do they have a clearly defined application? Does the equipment perform as expected? Do system services receive sustained remuneration? Does genuine demand support the expansion plans? Aggregate demand can create opportunities. An individual business still has to pass through those stages before the opportunity becomes earnings. Growth in manufacturing scale, read together with effective demand at the point of use, gives a fuller account of storage’s industrial value.
