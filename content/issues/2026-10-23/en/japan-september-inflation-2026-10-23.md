Japan’s Statistics Bureau reported on October 23 that September’s national consumer price index rose 2.1% year on year. CPI excluding fresh food also increased 2.1%, up from August’s 1.7%; excluding both fresh food and energy, inflation accelerated from 1.9% to 2.4%.[^1] Fuel alone therefore cannot explain price pressure, but the figures do not establish when the Bank of Japan will next change rates.

## What “core” actually excludes

Japan’s commonly used core CPI excludes fresh food but retains energy. The measure that also excludes energy still includes items such as processed food; it must not be described as excluding all food and energy. The Statistics Bureau explains that different exclusions help reduce particular sources of volatility, and that users must check coverage.[^2]

That distinction changes how to read the 2.4% figure. It shows rising average prices in the basket remaining after fresh food and energy are removed, but does not independently establish that strong domestic demand caused the increase. Food processing, transportation and other business costs can still feed into the remaining items. Removing an input’s direct price does not remove its indirect effect through supply chains.

Annual rates compare price levels twelve months apart and are affected by the previous year’s base. Acceleration can reflect recent price increases, changes in the comparison base, or both. Assessing persistence requires consecutive monthly movements, the breadth of increases and firms’ pricing behaviour. One annual reading cannot establish that the whole economy is overheating.

## The 2% target is not an automatic trigger

The Bank of Japan’s price-stability target is 2% annual CPI inflation.[^3] It does not mean that every month above 2% requires an immediate rate increase. Policy seeks to influence future activity and prices, so decisions require judgments about durability and the time needed for different instruments to work.

The relevant existing policy decision came on September 18: a 7–2 vote set the uncollateralised overnight call-rate target at around 1.25%, effective September 24.[^4] That decision preceded this release of nationwide September CPI. It cannot be presented as an immediate response to the October 23 data.

The September meeting’s summary of opinions, published on October 1, showed differing judgments about underlying inflation and economic strength.[^5] It is neither a new vote nor a guarantee of the next action. The latest figures add evidence for policymakers to weigh rather than provide a substitute for their eventual decision.

## Similar price increases can create different policy problems

If higher prices mainly reflect imported input costs while household incomes lag, businesses may face pressure on both margins and sales. If wages, service demand and pricing power develop a more durable interaction, inflation may prove more persistent. These mechanisms can coexist, and a single exclusion-based index cannot separate them fully.

Whether wage gains become real purchasing power, whether service-price increases are broad, and whether consumption can withstand higher prices are therefore important variables. This is an assessment framework, not a claim that September CPI has verified all those conditions. Replacing that assessment with the phrase “above target” conflates an observed number with an economic mechanism that still needs testing.

## How firms and households experience changing rates

The Bank of Japan’s explanation of monetary transmission links market and bank funding costs to business and household borrowing, and then to investment, consumption and prices.[^6] That chain does not imply that every loan reprices on the same day, or by the same amount, after a policy-rate change.

Fixed-rate and floating-rate contracts adjust differently. A company’s need to refinance soon also changes when pressure appears. For households, returns on deposits and borrowing expenses can move in different directions for their budgets. Assessing the effect requires looking at assets and liabilities rather than depicting a rate rise as a universal loss of income or a universal benefit.

Exchange rates add uncertainty. In an otherwise unchanged scenario, a stronger yen would lower the local-currency cost of imports priced in foreign currencies. But overseas rates, expectations and risk appetite also affect exchange rates. A CPI release therefore does not guarantee yen appreciation, and an unverified claim about that day’s market moves cannot establish the transmission.

For trades funded in yen and invested in assets denominated in other currencies, both funding costs and exchange-rate changes affect returns. A narrower interest-rate gap combined with yen appreciation could increase pressure to close positions, but leverage, hedging and position structures determine the actual scale. This is a risk mechanism, not evidence that the September figures have already triggered a global carry-trade unwind.

The clearest signal in this report is the simultaneous acceleration of two commonly used exclusion-based measures. It strengthens the case for examining inflation’s persistence, while the policy timetable still depends on wages, demand and financial conditions. Separating published data, the earlier rate decision and future scenarios shows which risks have become more relevant and which conclusions remain premature.
