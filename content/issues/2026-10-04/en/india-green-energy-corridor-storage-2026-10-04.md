India's cabinet approved the Green Energy Corridor Phase III scheme on September 30. Investment of about 1.864 trillion rupees is planned, to strengthen transmission networks within states and union territories and to install 50 gigawatt-hours of battery storage. Under the arrangements the government announced, the projects will support the connection and evacuation of up to 135 gigawatts of renewable power, with completion targeted for fiscal year 2032–2033. This is a construction program for the years ahead. The capacity concerned should not be treated as already operational.[^1]

The funding plan lists 136,378 crore rupees for intra-state transmission and 50,000 crore rupees for battery storage. Central government support of about 540.8 billion rupees should not be added on top of the project total. The announced total of 186,405 crore exceeds the sum of the transmission and storage components, 186,378 crore, by 27 crore (270 million rupees). No explanation is given. The original figures are retained here, with no attempt to fill in the difference.[^1]

## Why the grid and storage need to be built together

The policy comes as India's demand for electricity keeps growing, and as solar output fails to line up with evening load. An IEA analysis published in June noted that peak demand reached 270 gigawatts on May 21 this year, well above approximately 180 gigawatts in 2019. Solar power helps cover the daytime. Air-conditioning use stays high on summer nights, and after sunset the grid still has to provide enough electricity.[^2]

Storage and transmission have to be discussed together for that reason. Transmission decides where electricity can be sent. Storage decides when it can be released. Adding solar capacity does not, by itself, take the pressure off the evening. Building transmission lines does not, by itself, shift midday generation into the night. The IEA considers that batteries can cut peak demand and ease the pressure on coal-fired plants to ramp up quickly at sunset.[^2]

On demand over the medium term, the IEA's Electricity 2026 report projects average annual growth of about 6.4% in India's electricity demand from 2026 to 2030, with annual consumption increasing by more than 570 terawatt-hours over the five-year period. That is a forecast, not growth already realized. It does indicate that new transmission has to carry both new generation and new loads. In a power system growing this fast, a delay in the grid can undercut the practical value of a new generation project.[^3]

## From an approved plan to actual operation

How the scheme is carried out is its own question. The government has assigned state transmission companies to coordinate delivery. New transmission projects are to be procured through tariff-based competitive bidding, and upgrades to existing facilities handled under a cost-plus mechanism.[^1] One mechanism is competition for new projects. The other is modernization of the network already built. Tenders, the coordination of construction projects and the dates assets are actually commissioned will show whether the plan is moving. A comparison of approved budgets will not show that.

The units are easy to mix up. 135 gigawatts is the renewable power capacity the scheme is meant to support. 50 gigawatt-hours is the energy the batteries can store. The two cannot be added together, and they cannot be compared directly in size. As published, the announcement is not enough to calculate how many hours of additional electricity supply the country would gain. And 135 gigawatts should not be read as an equivalent amount of power-plant capacity built directly under this scheme.

The plan matters because it ties an expansion of generation more closely to whether electricity is available. Reliable supply, if it follows, will depend on whether transmission and storage are matched to demand, on when they come online, and on how much they actually improve curtailment and supply at the peak once they are running. Approval of the investment is the start. The system's operating record is what the policy can be judged on later.
