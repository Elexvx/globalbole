*Revised October 15, 2026: expanded the evidence on electricity supply and cash flow, and clarified the limits of long-term forecasts.*

PwC's central scenario projects US$31.6 trillion in cumulative global data-centre investment through 2050.[^1]

For any one project, the nearer questions are when it can start delivering a service that customers pay for, and how the bills get met before that day. Electricity availability decides whether the equipment can run. Financial statements separate money earned from customers from money put in by lenders or shareholders. Laid next to each other, those two views measure the distance between construction spending and a business that can support itself.[^2][^3]

## A measured starting point and a conditional outlook

The International Energy Agency's April 2025 report estimated that global data-centre investment reached about US$500 billion in 2024, nearly twice its 2022 level. Electricity consumption was put at 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, about 1.5% of the world total. These are estimates for a year already past. They cover data centres in general, rather than AI alone.[^2]

The same report's Base Case projected electricity use of about 945 TWh in 2030. AI was the main driver of the growth, with other digital services alongside. The figure is a scenario published in 2025. It is not a measurement of demand already delivered, and it is not an updated reading of 2026 activity. Electricity consumption cannot, on its own, show customer revenue, margins or the return on capital used to provide the service.[^2][^3]

The range is wide. Across the IEA's cases, data-centre electricity demand in 2035 ran from 700 to 1,700 TWh. Adoption, the efficiency of hardware and of models, and bottlenecks in the energy sector all shifted the results. Those cases are alternative assumptions. They are not a confidence interval, and they are not a guarantee of demand for any particular operator.[^2]

## The power connection has its own timetable

A modest share of the world total can still hide a tight local constraint. The IEA found that nearly half of US data-centre capacity was concentrated in five regional clusters. It also estimated that around 20% of planned projects could face delays unless electricity-system risks were addressed. That is a conditional assessment of exposure to delay. It is not a count of projects already postponed.[^2]

In advanced economies, the report says, building transmission lines can take four to eight years. Waits for transformers and cables have been getting longer. Buying servers and finishing a building do not establish when the site will have usable power. What counts is the connection schedule, the capacity available, and the dependencies that have to be finished before service begins.[^2]

The consequence for cash is conditional, and it is concrete. If customer receipts begin later while construction or financing payments still fall due, the project needs cash to cover the interval. Whether it has enough depends on its own payment terms and funding arrangements. The IEA's system-wide findings do not establish that a named company is short of cash. They do not establish that every delayed project will fail.[^2][^3]

## Revenue, profit and cash answer different questions

The US Securities and Exchange Commission's guide to financial statements explains that an income statement reports revenue and the expenses deducted to arrive at profit. A cash-flow statement tracks cash coming in and cash going out. Profit and the generation of cash are related. They are not interchangeable.[^3]

The distinction matters once the equipment is expensive. Depreciation spreads an asset's cost across the periods in which it is used. Buying machinery can require a cash outflow under investing activities. In the cash-flow reconciliation, depreciation is added back because it is non-cash for that period. The adjustment does not hand back money already spent on equipment, and it does not pay for the next purchase.[^3]

The guide keeps operating, investing and financing cash flows apart. Borrowing, or issuing shares, can increase the cash available for construction. Repaying a loan uses cash. Applied to an infrastructure operator, a rising cash balance is not enough to show that customers are paying for the expansion. The source of the cash matters, and so does the timing of what will fall due later.[^3]

## Efficiency changes the calculation, not just the electricity bill

The IEA's High Efficiency Case put 2035 data-centre electricity demand about 20% below its Base Case. The comparison holds to the assumptions in that report. It is not a forecast of a matching reduction in industry revenue. More efficient models or hardware can change how much electricity a workload needs. They do not decide what customers will pay for it.[^2]

The report also identified ways to ease integration: siting facilities where the grid has capacity, and using servers, onsite generation or storage more flexibly. Flexibility has trade-offs. The IEA noted that interrupting a highly capital-intensive AI facility can be costly. Spare computing capacity and backup assets create options. They do not make an electricity constraint disappear.[^2]

For a project, the findings lead to a linked set of questions. What service can be delivered with the power available? What do the operating costs look like? What receipts are left once those costs are paid? A fall in energy per task is useful evidence. The commercial effect depends on volume, on pricing and on the cost of providing the service. This applies the energy and accounting evidence already in hand. It is not a new market-size forecast.[^2][^3]

## What would demonstrate a sustainable operating model?

A review worth doing would set the commissioning record next to the accounts. Capacity that is actually receiving power. Services sold. Revenue and operating expenses. Cash generated by operations. Purchases of long-lived assets. Money raised, and money repaid. The measures should refer to the same business and to comparable periods. An industry-wide electricity scenario cannot substitute for a project's accounts.[^2][^3]

The SEC guide also stresses the notes to the financial statements and management's discussion of trends, risks and uncertainties. Those disclosures help explain the accounting choices, and why reported profit differs from cash flow. They give context for judging an expansion. No single figure settles the question.[^3]

The test that matters in practice is whether a facility can deliver a paid service on a timetable its funding can support, and whether operations can carry the continuing costs of that service. A delay in power can weaken the case. Efficiency, and more flexible operation, may improve it. The evidence supports examining those conditions project by project. It does not justify a blanket verdict on the commercial value of AI infrastructure.[^2][^3]
