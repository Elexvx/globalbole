*Updated October 15, 2026: clarified that the company commitments are voluntary, while the executive order governs federal terminology.*

The September 29 White House meeting put corporate controls and internal and external review at the centre of a voluntary AI safety commitment. Speaking after the meeting with President Donald Trump and industry leaders, House Speaker Mike Johnson said companies were taking responsibility for their technology and that Congress would continue examining the issue. His account described expectations for industry conduct, rather than a completed assessment of any company's safety performance.[^7]

## What the companies signed, and what officials promised

Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, xAI and Nvidia pledged internal controls, internal checks, independent external assessments and independent board committees.[^1]

Johnson presented the commitment as a way to build public trust while preserving American technological leadership. He said the White House and Congress would continue guiding development and that lawmakers would keep assessing and deliberating. Those are political commitments about the government's continuing role. His remarks did not announce a new federal audit office, a timetable for publishing company findings or a procedure through which the public could challenge an assessment.[^7]

That distinction matters for evaluating the meeting's outcome. An official declaration that companies will take ownership of safety gives readers a stated objective. It does not supply the test records, review methods or evidence of remedial action needed to judge whether that objective has been achieved. NIST's existing risk-management framework offers a more concrete way to organise those questions, but adopting its vocabulary is not evidence of implementation.[^7][^6]

Reuters reported conflicting views on federal auditor oversight and existing laws’ preventive effect.[^2]

## A separate order changes federal terminology

Executive Order 14434, also dated September 29, addresses the language used by the executive branch. To the extent permitted by law, it directs agencies to replace “artificial intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents and other non-statutory documents. Section 2(b) expressly says this does not require changes to previously issued regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants or other historical documents.[^5]

The distinction between a name and a legal definition is equally important. For the order's implementation, section 3(a) retains the technologies and systems covered by the existing AI definition in 15 USC 9401(3), except where the law provides otherwise. The science and technology adviser has 60 days to submit proposed legislative language, possible related amendments and recommendations for further executive action. That is a proposal assignment; the order does not say Congress has already enacted a replacement definition.[^5]

The executive instruction therefore has a different addressee and purpose from Johnson's description of voluntary industry reviews. It governs federal agencies' terminology within existing legal limits. Its general provisions preserve agencies' statutory authority and do not create a privately enforceable right or benefit. Reading it as proof that a model has reached a scientifically measured capability threshold would go beyond the definition the order actually uses.[^5][^7]

In a Reuters/Ipsos poll, 73% of respondents feared inadequate corporate prevention of serious societal harm.[^4]

Technical risk assessment asks what harms a system could produce in its operating context, and how those risks are measured and managed. NIST specifically cautions that difficulty measuring a risk does not establish that it is either high or low.[^6]

## What would make oversight assessable

NIST's January 2023 AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary guidance for organisations designing, developing, deploying or using AI. It is not the September 2026 agreement and does not certify the signatories. Its GOVERN 2.1 and 2.3 provisions put documented responsibilities, communication lines and executive responsibility at the centre of risk decisions. Applied as a comparison here, they suggest looking for named decision-makers and clear responsibility for handling findings, rather than relying solely on the existence of a review team.[^6]

Independence also needs a precise description. MEASURE 1.3 calls for regular assessments involving internal experts who were not front-line developers and/or independent assessors. It is not a universal requirement that every evaluation be outsourced. MEASURE 1.1 and 2.1 ask organisations to document their chosen metrics, test sets and tools, as well as risks that cannot or will not be measured. These distinctions help readers assess what a review can actually establish and where its coverage ends.[^6]

Finally, MANAGE 4.1–4.3 addresses monitoring after deployment, incident response and recovery, changes to systems, continuing improvement and communication of incidents and errors to relevant parties, including affected communities. A useful follow-on assessment would therefore examine how findings lead to action and how those actions are documented. This is an application of NIST's guidance, not a claim that the companies have already disclosed such records or adopted every provision.[^6]

The central question remains whether the voluntary expectations described at the White House will be accompanied by assessable evidence. The official account explains the intended direction; the executive order explains a terminology change; the NIST framework supplies evaluation questions. None of these documents, on its own, establishes that the promised company safeguards are operating successfully.[^7][^5][^6]
