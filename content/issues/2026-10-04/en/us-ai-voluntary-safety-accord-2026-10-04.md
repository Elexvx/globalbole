*Updated October 15, 2026: clarified that the company commitments are voluntary, while the executive order governs federal terminology.*

The White House meeting on September 29 put corporate controls, together with internal and external review, at the centre of a voluntary commitment on AI safety. Speaking after the meeting with President Donald Trump and industry leaders, House Speaker Mike Johnson said companies were taking responsibility for their technology and that Congress would keep examining the issue. His account described expectations for industry conduct. It was not a completed assessment of any company's safety performance.[^7]

## What the companies signed, and what officials promised

Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, xAI and Nvidia pledged internal controls, internal checks, independent external assessments and independent board committees.[^1]

Johnson presented the commitment as a way to build public trust while keeping American technological leadership. The White House and Congress, he said, would go on guiding development, and lawmakers would keep assessing and deliberating. Those are political commitments about a role the government means to continue. His remarks did not announce a new federal audit office, a timetable for publishing company findings, or a procedure through which the public could challenge an assessment.[^7]

That gap is what the meeting's outcome has to be judged against. An official declaration that companies will take ownership of safety gives readers a stated objective. It does not supply the test records, the review methods or the evidence of remedial action needed to judge whether the objective has been met. NIST's existing framework for managing risk is a more concrete way to organise those questions. Adopting its vocabulary is not evidence of implementation.[^7][^6]

Reuters reported conflicting views on federal auditor oversight and on the preventive effect of existing laws.[^2]

## A separate order changes federal terminology

Executive Order 14434, also dated September 29, addresses the language used by the executive branch. To the extent permitted by law, it directs agencies to replace “artificial intelligence” and “AI” with “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in correspondence, public communications, websites, reports, policy documents and other non-statutory documents. Section 2(b) expressly says this does not require changes to previously issued regulations, presidential actions, contracts, grants or other historical documents.[^5]

A new name is not a new legal definition. For the order's implementation, section 3(a) retains the technologies and systems covered by the existing AI definition in 15 USC 9401(3), except where the law provides otherwise. The science and technology adviser has 60 days to submit proposed legislative language, possible related amendments and recommendations for further executive action. That is an assignment to propose. The order does not say Congress has already enacted a replacement definition.[^5]

The instruction to agencies and Johnson's description of voluntary industry reviews have different addressees, and different purposes. The order governs federal terminology, inside limits the law already sets. Its general provisions preserve the statutory authority of agencies. They do not create a right or benefit a private party can enforce. To read the order as proof that a model has crossed a scientifically measured capability threshold would go beyond the definition the order actually uses.[^5][^7]

In a Reuters/Ipsos poll, 73% of respondents feared inadequate corporate prevention of serious societal harm.[^4]

Technical risk assessment asks what harms a system could produce in the setting where it operates, and how those risks are measured and managed. NIST specifically cautions that difficulty measuring a risk does not establish that it is either high or low.[^6]

## What would make oversight assessable

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, from January 2023, is voluntary guidance for organisations that design, develop, deploy or use AI. It is not the September 2026 agreement, and it does not certify the signatories. GOVERN 2.1 and 2.3 put documented responsibilities, lines of communication and executive responsibility at the centre of decisions on risk. Applied here as a comparison, they suggest looking for named decision-makers, and for a clear responsibility for handling findings, rather than relying solely on the existence of a review team.[^6]

Independence needs to be described precisely. MEASURE 1.3 calls for regular assessments involving internal experts who were not front-line developers and/or independent assessors. It is not a universal requirement that every evaluation be outsourced. MEASURE 1.1 and 2.1 ask organisations to document the metrics, test sets and tools they chose, and also the risks that cannot or will not be measured. Those distinctions show what a review can actually establish, and where its coverage ends.[^6]

MANAGE 4.1–4.3 covers what happens after deployment: monitoring, incident response and recovery, changes to systems, continuing improvement, and communication of incidents and errors to the relevant parties, including affected communities. A useful follow-on assessment would examine how findings lead to action, and how that action is documented. This is an application of NIST's guidance. It is not a claim that the companies have already disclosed such records, or that they have adopted every provision.[^6]

Whether the voluntary expectations described at the White House will be accompanied by evidence that can be assessed is still the open question. The official account explains the direction intended. The executive order explains a change of terminology. The NIST framework supplies questions for an evaluation. None of these documents, on its own, establishes that the company safeguards being promised are operating successfully.[^7][^5][^6]
