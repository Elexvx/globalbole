Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky told Reuters in an interview on October 3 that Ukraine would intensify strikes on Russian oil-refining facilities, in response to what he described as an expansion of Russian air attacks on Ukrainian cities. He also stressed that Ukraine would not attack civilian targets indiscriminately. Ukrainian intelligence services, he said, had obtained documents on Russia's new approach to attacks. Neither the document nor its contents had been independently verified in public reporting.[^1]

## Bridge damage affects how the city functions

The remarks came amid repeated attacks on Kyiv's transport links. The Kyiv Independent reported that Russian forces struck the Northern Bridge across the Dnipro River early on October 3. Mayor Vitali Klitschko said the road surface and the trolleybus overhead wires were damaged, two people were injured and traffic was halted for a time. The Southern Bridge had also faced successive drone attacks. At the time of publication, the full extent of damage to the Northern Bridge remained unclear. A disruption to traffic should not be equated with destruction of the bridge itself.[^2]

What an attack on a bridge does depends on more than the structural damage. Kyiv's two banks rely on the river crossings for commuting and transport. The bridge can still be standing while temporary closures, and traffic restrictions during alerts, interrupt the way the city works. Aviation experts interviewed by the newspaper said the attacks may have been intended to obstruct movement, disrupt logistics and create psychological pressure. That is expert analysis of possible intent. It is not a confirmed statement of Russian operational objectives.[^2]

## Statements about a response do not establish verified damage

Zelensky linked the planned intensification of refinery strikes to Russia's sources of war funding. Reuters reported that the Kremlin could not provide comment that day. Russia's Foreign Ministry, in a statement urging foreigners to leave Kyiv, said it would continue what it described as retaliatory strikes. Both Russia and Ukraine deny deliberately targeting civilians.[^1] Claims of a “new doctrine,” “retaliation” and the reasons for target selection need to remain clearly attributed to the speakers who made them.

Ukrainian strikes on Russian oil facilities did not start with this statement. In an announcement on October 2, the Security Service of Ukraine said it had joined other forces in striking four oil infrastructure sites in the Samara and Volgograd regions, including pumping stations and the Volgograd refinery, and reported fires at the sites.[^3] The announcement establishes that Ukraine publicly claimed these operations. It cannot, by itself, establish the actual damage at every facility, how long any shutdown lasted, or the production lost.

## The civilian effects of attacks on energy facilities cannot be overlooked

Attacks on energy facilities also raise legal and humanitarian questions that a political statement cannot close. An analysis by legal advisers at the International Committee of the Red Cross notes that whether energy infrastructure constitutes a military objective must be assessed in the specific circumstances. Even where a facility has a military use, an attack must still comply with the principle of proportionality and with the obligation to take precautions. Reasonably foreseeable indirect harm, when power cuts reach services such as drinking water, health care and sanitation, must be included in that assessment.[^4]

A declaration that civilians will not be attacked does not, by itself, establish that a particular strike will cause no civilian harm. Responding on the grounds of having been attacked does not lift the relevant obligations either. Targets, the damage actually done and the capacity to recover have to be considered together. Images of fires cannot, on their own, determine military effectiveness. Labeling a facility “strategic” does not remove the need to examine its civilian functions.

Two threads will shape what happens next: whether Kyiv's bridges and other public facilities can return to reliable operation, and how lasting the impact has been on oil facilities inside Russia. Without verified data on shutdowns, repairs and markets, the statement cannot yet be used to calculate changes in diesel or global oil prices. What can be confirmed is that attacks, defenses and public threats centered on infrastructure are continuing. The risks borne by urban residents, and by energy systems, need continued attention.
