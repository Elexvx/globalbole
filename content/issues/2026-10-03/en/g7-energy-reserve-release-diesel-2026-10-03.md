The G7 is preparing to release 100 million barrels of oil reserves over four months, with a substantial diesel release scheduled for the first 20 days. A leaders' statement released by the French presidency on October 2 said the action, coordinated by the International Energy Agency (IEA), would begin immediately. It did not specify the number of barrels of diesel to be released in the initial period.[^1]

The arrangement does two things. It increases the stocks available for release, and it adjusts refinery production schedules. How much pressure that takes off will depend on the form in which fuel arrives, when it arrives and where it goes.

## Front-loading diesel targets deliveries of refined products

The statement uses the phrase “frontloaded substantial diesel release,” a substantial release of diesel early in the process. Among the supporting steps: G7 refinery maintenance is to be coordinated so shutdowns are not simultaneous, and utilization rates are to be raised temporarily where that is feasible. The parties also encourage cooperation with countries that have substantial refining capacity, to increase supplies of refined products.[^1]

Crude oil sitting in a reserve, and diesel ready to be delivered and used, are at different points in the supply chain. In the United States, for example, most diesel is refined from crude oil, and it is widely used in freight transport, agriculture and construction equipment.[^2] For businesses that rely on those activities, barrels of crude in storage and the ability to secure enough fuel in the near term are closely related. They are distinct questions.

Moving diesel releases forward is about deliveries now. Keeping refineries from shutting down together is about the production that follows. A reading that stops at “100 million barrels” misses the steps that decide whether the policy works.

## The 100 million barrels cannot simply be added to earlier pledges

On March 11, the IEA announced that its 32 member countries had agreed to make 400 million barrels of emergency stocks available to the market, with release schedules set by each country's circumstances. That was a commitment made in March. It cannot automatically be treated as deliveries completed by October.[^3]

The latest G7 statement calls for tracking implementation of the March commitments, while explicitly taking account of the portion already fulfilled.[^1] Adding the earlier 400 million barrels to the latest 100 million produces 500 million barrels. That arithmetic confuses commitments with execution, and it is not a measure of additional supply. Treating the entire 100 million barrels as diesel would go beyond the statement as well.

Country-by-country release schedules, the composition of the products, and the link between this arrangement and earlier commitments are what show how much extra supply has actually reached the market. Until those details are fully disclosed, the total is a way to grasp the scale of the action. It is a weak basis for calculating an immediate increase in supply.

## Reserves buy time, but the effect still depends on implementation

Releasing inventories can provide a buffer. How large a buffer depends on the speed of deliveries and on where they go. If fuel cannot reach the places that need it in time, even a large pledge will do little to ease a local shortage immediately. Releases can go smoothly and still draw the reserves down. Replenishment has to be arranged too.

The G7 has asked the IEA to submit a report on implementation and impact within 20 days, together with recommendations including replenishment.[^1] The report is a checkpoint: whether commitments were delivered on time, whether releases focused on the products and regions most in need, and whether refinery coordination improved supply.

Every stage can shift the arrival time, from the release of stocks and refinery processing through cross-border transport and final distribution. The impact at the retail level may take longer still to be felt. Actual diesel release volumes, delivery schedules, refinery utilization and inventory changes are the things to track. The statement on its own does not support a conclusion that oil prices have peaked.

This round of action sets a relatively clear direction for coordination in the short term. The question now is whether commitments on supply turn into fuel that businesses and households can actually buy.
