A battery that stores electricity from rooftop solar panels and electricity bought from the grid raises an awkward question when power is fed back: how should subsidies and charges be calculated? The MiSpeL rules, adopted by Germany's Federal Network Agency on October 1, take up those details. They decide whether storage and bidirectional charging can participate in the electricity market.[^1]

## Clearer accounting is finally available for mixed electricity storage

According to the agency, retaining the relevant subsidies under the Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) previously required storage facilities to store only renewable electricity. Bidirectional charging points were shut out of that model. The restriction was more than a technicality. Equipment that can both charge and discharge still cannot settle subsidies and charges accurately if the sources of the electricity cannot be told apart.[^1]

MiSpeL opens a path for mixed use. It distinguishes renewable electricity eligible for subsidies from electricity fed back to the grid that qualifies for credits. Eligible renewable electricity held temporarily in storage can still receive subsidies. Electricity drawn from the grid and later fed back can qualify, under the rules, for offsets against the relevant levies and network charges. For an operator, that is a clearer basis for using the same equipment for self-consumption and for trading in the market.

Eligibility has a condition attached. The new arrangements apply to users who sell electricity directly on the market, not to those receiving fixed feed-in tariffs. Households that already have solar panels and electric vehicles have to check their sales arrangements first. They cannot simply assume an existing contract lets them participate.[^2]

## Precise metering and the simplified option each involve trade-offs

The precise differentiation option covers installations of all sizes. Digital metering identifies the flows. It generally requires two meters, although one may suffice in certain special cases. The simplified flat-rate option is intended for small solar systems of up to 30 kilowatts peak, covering households and small businesses, and can generally calculate the relevant amounts with one meter at the connection point. For a small user, fewer metering upgrades make it easier to keep the cost of taking part in hand.[^1]

The fee relief has clear limits. Electricity used to drive an electric vehicle, and the associated losses, remain subject to levies and network charges. Electricity charged elsewhere cannot qualify for EEG subsidies simply because it is fed back through a home charging point. The point of the distinctions is to settle eligible grid exports and ordinary consumption separately.[^2]

## Once settlement is possible, the returns still need to add up

What MiSpeL changes, in practice, is the link between what the equipment can do and how the market settles the power. A battery that charges and discharges in both directions is only the start. Operators also need to know which electricity remains eligible for subsidies, which charges can be offset, and who handles metering and payment. Clearer rules make it easier for a service provider to turn a technical setup into a product a customer can sign.

Returns do not follow on their own. The gap between charging at low prices and exporting at high prices still has to cover equipment, metering, service fees, energy losses and the cost of using the battery. How much capacity a household can offer is also limited by travel and by the hours available to charge. Someone comparing offers should ask how settlement works, who bears the costs and how the revenue is shared, rather than stopping at the earnings advertised.

Putting the rules into effect will take time. The transition period runs until the end of September 2027. Earlier use requires agreement from the grid operator and the metering-point operator. The simplified flat-rate option must also await European Commission state-aid approval for its legal basis.[^1]

Adopting the rules is a real step. The questions after that are narrower: when operators start taking applications, how compatibility and metering requirements are actually implemented, and whether products appear with terms a customer can read and costs that can be calculated. Bidirectional charging becomes an ordinary option for more users only once those pieces fit.
