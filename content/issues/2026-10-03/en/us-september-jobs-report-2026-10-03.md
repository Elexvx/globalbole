US nonfarm employment rose by 29,000 in September. The unemployment rate was 4.2%. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) report released on October 2 also revised the combined job gains for July and August downward by 60,000. The month's increase, set next to those revisions, still describes a labor market with weak hiring growth and a split performance across industries.[^1]

Over the preceding 12 months, nonfarm employment had risen by an average of 45,000 jobs a month. September's increase was below that pace. The BLS nevertheless described the monthly changes in both nonfarm employment and the unemployment rate as having “changed little.” The phrase is a reminder. A single month has to be read against the longer trend, and against statistical uncertainty.

## Health care continues to add jobs as finance adjusts

Health care added 17,000 jobs in September, below its average monthly increase of 33,000 over the previous 12 months. Inside the sector, ambulatory health care services added 13,000 and hospitals added 12,000, while nursing and residential care facilities lost 9,000. Construction added 11,000 jobs. Manufacturing added 9,000. The BLS also noted that employment changed little overall across the major industries that month. These increases and decreases are not yet enough to establish a turning point in each industry.[^1]

Employment in financial activities fell by 7,000. Since a recent peak in May 2025, the sector has lost 129,000 jobs, including 90,000 in insurance and related activities. A small increase in the national total does not mean the search for work is getting easier in every industry.

## Beyond the unemployment rate, look at hours and the length of job searches

About 7.1 million people were unemployed in September. Roughly 1.9 million had been unemployed for 27 weeks or longer, 27.1% of all unemployed people. About 4.5 million were working part time involuntarily because their hours had been reduced or they could not find full-time jobs. Both groups changed little in size that month. They still describe problems the unemployment rate leaves out. Finding a job, securing enough hours and ending a long search are different difficulties.[^1]

The labor force participation rate was 61.8%, and the employment-population ratio was 59.2%. Where the numbers come from matters. Nonfarm payroll figures come from an employer survey. The unemployment rate and the related indicators come from a household survey. Coverage differs, so job gains cannot be directly netted against changes in the number of unemployed people.

## How does the 60,000-job downward revision change the recent picture?

July's employment change was revised from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000. August's increase was revised from 162,000 to 133,000. Combined, the downward revision is 60,000. Additional survey responses and recalculated seasonal factors both feed into the monthly revisions. September's figures are still preliminary estimates.[^1]

On pay, average hourly earnings for private nonfarm employees were $37.81, up 0.1% from the previous month and 3.0% from a year earlier. The average workweek remained at 34.4 hours. Wages and hours did not both decline sharply. Hourly earnings here are a nominal measure. Any reading of purchasing power also has to be set against price changes.

## The evidence points more to slow growth than a sudden downturn

Average monthly job gains over the past year were already modest, and September's unemployment rate remained inside the narrow 4.1%–4.3% range seen since March. The closer description of the evidence is “limited employment growth”, set against “a sudden deterioration in the labor market.” The downward revisions to the previous two months do make recent hiring look less strong than first reported.

Sampling uncertainty limits what one month can support. At the 90% confidence level usually used by the BLS, a monthly change in nonfarm employment of about 122,000 is needed to meet the threshold for statistical significance. A gain of 29,000 can help in tracking the trend. On its own, it is not well suited to strong conclusions about recession or interest rates.[^2]

The next employment report is expected on November 6. Alongside the new job figures, it will be worth seeing whether the revised September figures, long-term unemployment, labor force participation and working hours change together. If those indicators corroborate one another, that will be more persuasive than one striking monthly increase.
