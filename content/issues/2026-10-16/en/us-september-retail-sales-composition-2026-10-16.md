Retailers and restaurants took in more money in September. The interesting part is which tills it landed in. Advance estimates from the Census Bureau on October 15 put U.S. retail and food services sales at $745.0 billion, up 0.8% from August and 6.3% from September 2025. Seasonal patterns are adjusted for, and so are holiday and trading-day differences. Price changes are not. The sampling margin of error is ±0.4 percentage points on the monthly change and ±0.5 points on the annual change, both at the 90% confidence level.[^1]

Gasoline stations rose 6.4% in the month. Strip them out and sales increased just 0.2%. Dollar amounts, the mix of industries and prices have to be read together before the month can be called a broad strengthening of demand. The industry mix says where a growth signal applies. Prices and quantities say whether customers bought more goods.[^1]

## Gasoline stations account for most of the dollar increase

Table 1 has the dollars. Total retail and food services sales went from $739.322 billion in August to $745.021 billion in September, a gain of $5.699 billion. Gasoline stations went from $63.950 billion to $68.021 billion, a gain of $4.071 billion. Divide one published change by the other and gasoline stations account for approximately 71.4% of the month’s extra dollars. The arithmetic is ours, worked from official figures. The Census Bureau did not publish it as a cause, and it has no confidence interval of its own.[^1]

Everyone else, taken together, still grew. Sales excluding gasoline stations rose from $675.372 billion to $677.000 billion, or about 0.2%. Leave out only motor vehicle and parts dealers and sales rose 0.9%. Leave out those dealers and gasoline stations, and growth is back at 0.2%. The dealers themselves recorded a 0.2% increase. The 0.9% figure that drops autos still contains the jump at the pump. Drop both categories and the remaining retail trade is easier to see.[^1]

Revenue at a gasoline station is not the volume of gasoline sold. Establishments are classified by their main business, so sales of one kind of store are not nationwide sales of one product. Price, quantity and the mix of goods can all move station revenue, and the report does not split them apart. The 71.4% share cannot be rewritten as a finding that fuel prices explained 71.4% of retail growth.[^2]

## Industries outside gasoline stations diverge

The seasonally adjusted point estimates for the month show furniture and home furnishings stores up 1.3%, electronics and appliance stores up 0.6%, food and beverage stores up 0.4%, and health and personal care stores up 0.7%. Nonstore retailers gained 0.3%. Food services and drinking places rose 1.1%. Building material and garden equipment and supplies dealers fell 0.6%. Sporting goods, hobby, musical instrument and book stores fell 0.5%. General merchandise stores slipped 0.1%. Miscellaneous store retailers declined 3.0%. Clothing and accessories stores were unchanged at the precision the release publishes.[^1]

A plus or a minus on that list is an industry estimate. It is not a certificate that the move is statistically significant. The Census Bureau warns, explicitly, that comparisons across industries have not been tested for significance. Putting furniture’s 1.3% above electronics’ 0.6% and calling the ranking a performance table would ignore how wide the sampling error can be. Counting the industries that rose would also ignore that some of them are much larger than others.[^1]

The month and the year can disagree. Health and personal care sales rose 0.7% from August and were still 1.2% below a year earlier. Sporting goods and the related store categories fell 0.5% in the month and remained up 9.6% on the year. The monthly figure is the latest step. The annual figure is where sales stand against a year ago. One observation cannot show whether a rebound becomes a recovery, or whether a decline sticks. That takes a run of them.[^1]

## Higher sales dollars do not necessarily mean more purchases

What the report measures is current-dollar sales. Seasonal adjustment handles patterns that come around every year, and differences in the calendar. Price adjustment handles purchasing power, or the number of units. Doing one is not doing the other. After seasonal adjustment, a higher price can still lift the dollar take. A lower price can let unit sales rise while the dollars barely move.[^1][^2]

Real retail growth needs prices matched to the sales categories, the weights and the period being compared. Subtracting some headline inflation rate from 0.8% will not produce it. Food is sold in places other than food stores, and other goods cross from one retail channel to another. Store revenue and consumption of a given commodity do not line up one for one.[^2]

The net is retail and food services businesses, not every service a household buys. Table 1 says current estimates cover businesses with paid employees only. Before the benchmark report published in April 2025, the advance estimates had included nonemployers. A long view should stay on one consistent, updated series. Stitching numbers from releases that are not compatible will not do.[^1]

## August's upward revision changes September's starting point

July-to-August growth was revised from 1.1% to 1.3%. The sampling margin attached to it moved from ±0.4 to ±0.1 percentage points. September’s 0.8% is measured from that revised August level. Sales were still rising, at a slower monthly rate than the revised August figure. They were not falling. A slowdown that persists would have to appear in the months that follow.[^1]

July–September sales ran 5.5% above the same three months of 2025, with a sampling margin of ±0.5 percentage points. That three-month comparison and September’s 6.3% rise on the year cover different windows. Read them separately. Both are nominal sales. Neither is consumption with prices taken out.[^1]

The monthly series was also revised on September 28, using historical corrections and the 2023 and 2024 Annual Integrated Economic Survey. That benchmark and the revision to August in this release are different layers of adjustment. Anyone using the series for a budget or a trend should note the vintage and look again when a newer one arrives. A number in an earlier announcement does not stay put.[^1]

## The 0.8% signal is clearer than some smaller movements

Apply the published margin and September’s overall monthly change has an approximate 90% confidence interval from a 0.4% increase to a 1.2% increase. Zero is outside it. The opening chart of the release marks the 0.2% increase that excludes autos and gasoline stations with an asterisk. That 90% interval includes zero, so the sample is not enough to conclude that the underlying population change differs from zero. The underlying change is the statistical quantity being estimated. It does not mean inflation-adjusted growth.[^1]

The two results can stand together. Total dollar sales look, on this evidence, as if they rose. The smaller rise in the narrower aggregate does not clear the same statistical bar. An interval that contains zero does not prove the change was zero, and it does not establish a decline.

Sampling error is not the whole of the uncertainty. The report also names nonresponse, incomplete coverage of businesses, and problems in recording, coding and processing. The ±0.4-point margin on the headline does not swallow all of those. It also does not describe a range inside which any one company’s sales must have moved.[^1]

## Why advance estimates change, and how businesses can use them

About 4,800 employer businesses in retail and food services are drawn from the larger monthly survey for the advance release. Inside each detailed industry, statisticians form a ratio from the weighted sales of firms that reported usable numbers for both the current month and the previous one. That ratio is linked to the previous month’s preliminary estimate from the bigger sample. Detailed industries are then added into broader groups. Most firms that do not respond are not imputed one by one in the advance survey. A limited number of influential companies may be given estimates based on how they have performed in the past.[^1]

The point of the design is to publish early. Later returns can move the number, and the method helps explain why. A buyer using the release should set it against the firm’s own orders, traffic, selling prices and inventory turnover. If the extra revenue is mostly a higher price on the same volume, stocking up in line with the dollar figures can leave goods unsold. If quantities keep rising across several channels, replenishment has a stronger case. That is a business judgment, conditional on the firm’s own books. The report does not find that inventories have built up.

A restaurant’s profit also needs its costs. Household purchasing power needs income. Financing conditions need something on credit. Put those next to the national industry figures and the company’s own records, and the question of whether the sales growth lasts becomes easier to judge.

## The next report must test whether growth spreads

October’s advance estimates are scheduled, the release says, for November 17 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Standard Time. The checks that would matter are whether September’s industry figures get revised, whether growth outside gasoline stations continues, whether the rise that excludes autos and gasoline picks up clearer statistical support, and whether prices and quantities, matched to each other, point to more goods actually being bought.[^1]

September’s conclusion is clear, and it is bounded. Nominal sales of U.S. retail and food services kept rising, and gasoline stations accounted for most of the extra dollars. Resilience in demand more broadly still has to be tested on the series that leave particular industries out, on the revisions still to come, and on price information that matches the sales.
