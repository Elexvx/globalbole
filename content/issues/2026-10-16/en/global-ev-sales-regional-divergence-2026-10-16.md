Global EV sales in September were 2.15 million, Benchmark says, 2% below a year earlier.[^1]

One growth rate is a thin way to judge how electrification is going. The International Energy Agency’s July market update had already picked out the awkward combination: global electric-car sales fell about 1% in the first half of 2026, yet their share of all new-car sales increased to 24%. Sales grew in more than 90 countries. That was not enough to offset the effect of shrinking large markets on the global total.[^2] Falling volumes, a rising market share and growth in individual regions can sit in the same set of figures.

September and the third quarter give a fresher look at that split. Vehicle categories, geographical coverage and reporting periods differ, so the numbers cannot simply be added up into one market table.

## European growth needs a country and vehicle-category lens

UK car registrations in September rose 12% from a year earlier. Battery-electric registrations reached 99,201, up 36.3%, and took 28.3% of new cars.[^3]

The European rise was already under way before September. The IEA’s July update found first-half electric-car sales in Europe 30% higher than a year earlier, with gains in Germany, the United Kingdom and France, among other markets. The observation is for the half-year. It is not a country-by-country reading of September, and it does not mean every European market grew at that pace.[^2]

Choice and price help explain why the regions look different. The IEA’s 2026 annual report found that Europe’s range of available electric-car models expanded considerably in 2025. In Germany, cheaper models also helped bring down the average price of a battery-electric car. Buyers in Europe still had fewer low-priced battery-electric models to choose from than combustion cars at similar prices.[^6] Growth in any one market can continue only if people can find vehicles that fit their budgets and their needs. A regional total cannot settle that question.

The category of vehicle matters here. Battery-electric cars, plug-in hybrids and conventional hybrids that cannot charge externally are different statistical categories. The IEA’s relevant electric-car analysis includes the first two. That scope has to be kept when its findings are set against figures covering battery-electric vehicles alone.[^6]

## China: volumes and penetration answer different questions

Passenger-car retail sales in China fell 28% in September from a year earlier. New-energy passenger cars fell 16.7% at retail, and their retail share was 67%.[^4]

Market share is a category’s proportion of the total market. Sales volume is how many vehicles were sold. If the overall market contracts faster, electric vehicles can lose sales while gaining share. A higher share does not automatically increase factory orders, dealers’ cash flow or company profits.

The mechanism was already visible earlier in the year. The IEA’s July report found that total Chinese car sales fell more than 20% in the first half, with conventional vehicles declining faster than electric cars. Electric cars accounted for more than 60% of new-car sales in the second quarter.[^2] Greater electrification and improving demand for every manufacturer are not the same claim. The first-half pattern does not, by itself, establish the causes of September’s changes.

Manufacturers also need to keep domestic retail sales, exports and wholesale shipments apart. Retail sales are closer to final purchases. Factory deliveries to distribution channels may first build inventory. A channel that has received the vehicles is not evidence that consumers have already paid for them and collected them.

## United States: examine the comparison behind the annual decline

The estimate for US battery-electric sales in the third quarter is 262,649, a year-on-year decline of 39.5%.[^5]

A year-on-year comparison needs last year’s market, not just this year’s. The IEA’s July report attributed some of the earlier US annual weakness to the expiry of the federal purchase tax credit at the end of the third quarter of 2025. It also recorded a recovery in second-quarter 2026 sales from the first quarter, while volumes remained below the previous year’s level.[^2] A sequential recovery and an annual decline can occur together. They describe different intervals.

If annual declines subsequently narrow, it will still be necessary to check whether current sales increased or the comparison base fell. Sales, market share and purchase costs, read across several periods, give a firmer basis for judging demand. The earlier sequential improvement cannot simply be extrapolated into continuing growth in future quarters.

## When do exports become overseas demand?

The regional split can also change how much manufacturers lean on foreign markets. The IEA’s July report found that Chinese electric-car exports grew considerably faster in the first half than overseas sales of those vehicles. It estimated that more than one million vehicles exported during its preceding 18-month observation window had not yet been registered as sales in destination-market statistics.[^2]

Shipping and delivery explain part of the timing gap between the two records. The IEA nevertheless judged that the size of the difference also suggested inventory accumulation in some destination markets.[^2] That was a midyear estimate, not a measurement of inventory at the end of September. The whole difference cannot be classified as unsellable vehicles.

The operating question follows from that gap. Overseas shipments improve factory utilisation on a lasting basis only if destination markets actually buy the cars. Vehicles that remain in distribution channels can bring discounting, storage and working-capital costs, and those costs can erode the gains from growth. That is an operating risk inferred from the distinction between shipments and sales. It is not a claim that a particular company is already making losses.

## What evidence is needed next?

A reading of the EV industry has to hold three facts in view together: whether consumers are buying more vehicles, whether EVs are taking share from other powertrains, and whether final demand is absorbing the extra production.

The IEA’s purchase-price research also shows why cheaper batteries need not show up, in proportion, as cheaper cars in the showroom. Vehicle size, specification, product mix and manufacturers’ pricing decisions all affect the outcome.[^6] Manufacturers and suppliers have to look at the structure of demand and the conditions for profit at the same time. When volumes, model mix, prices and inventory turnover point the same way, a temporary statistical fluctuation is easier to tell from a lasting change in operating conditions.
