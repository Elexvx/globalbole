Benchmark puts September global EV sales at 2.15 million, 2% below a year earlier.[^1]

One growth rate cannot capture the progress of electrification. The International Energy Agency’s July market update had already identified an important combination: global electric-car sales fell about 1% in the first half of 2026, yet their share of all new-car sales increased to 24%. Sales grew in more than 90 countries, but this did not offset the effect of shrinking large markets on the global total.[^2] Falling volumes, a rising market share and growth in individual regions can therefore coexist.

September and third-quarter statistics provide fresh perspectives on that divergence. Their vehicle categories, geographical coverage and reporting periods differ, so they cannot simply be combined into an additive market table.

## European growth needs a country and vehicle-category lens

UK September car registrations rose 12% year-on-year; battery-electric registrations reached 99,201, up 36.3%, accounting for 28.3% of new cars.[^3]

European growth did not begin that month. The IEA’s July update found that first-half electric-car sales in Europe were 30% higher than a year earlier, with increases in Germany, the United Kingdom and France, among other markets. This is a midyear observation. It does not replace a complete country-by-country September assessment or imply that every European market is growing at the same rate.[^2]

Vehicle availability and prices help explain regional differences. The IEA’s 2026 annual report found that Europe’s available electric-car model range expanded considerably in 2025. In Germany, the introduction of cheaper models also helped lower average battery-electric purchase prices. Nevertheless, buyers in Europe still had fewer low-priced battery-electric models to choose from than similarly priced combustion cars.[^6] Whether growth in a particular market can continue therefore depends partly on whether consumers can find vehicles suited to their budgets and needs; regional totals alone cannot establish that.

Vehicle categories matter here. Battery-electric cars, plug-in hybrids and conventional hybrids that cannot charge externally are different statistical categories. The IEA’s relevant electric-car analysis includes the first two. That scope must be retained when comparing its findings with figures covering battery-electric vehicles alone.[^6]

## China: volumes and penetration answer different questions

Chinese September passenger-car retail sales fell 28% year-on-year; new-energy passenger-car retail sales fell 16.7%, with a 67% retail share.[^4]

Market share measures a category’s proportion of the total market; sales volume measures how many vehicles were sold. If the overall market contracts faster, electric vehicles can lose sales while gaining share. Conversely, a higher share does not automatically increase factory orders, dealers’ cash flow or company profits.

Earlier data already illustrated this mechanism. The IEA’s July report found that total Chinese car sales fell more than 20% in the first half, with conventional vehicles declining faster than electric cars. Electric cars accounted for more than 60% of new-car sales in the second quarter.[^2] This explains why greater electrification cannot be equated with improving demand for every manufacturer. It does not, by itself, establish the causes of September’s changes.

Manufacturers also need to distinguish domestic retail sales, exports and wholesale shipments. Retail sales are closer to final purchases; factory deliveries to distribution channels may first build inventory. A channel receiving vehicles does not mean consumers have already paid for and collected them.

## United States: examine the comparison behind the annual decline

Estimated US third-quarter battery-electric sales total 262,649, down 39.5% year-on-year.[^5]

Year-on-year comparisons require the previous year’s market conditions. The IEA’s July report attributed some of the earlier US annual weakness to the expiry of the federal purchase tax credit at the end of the third quarter of 2025. It also recorded a recovery in second-quarter 2026 sales from the first quarter, while volumes remained below the previous year’s level.[^2] Sequential recovery and an annual decline can occur together because they describe different intervals.

If annual declines subsequently narrow, it will still be necessary to check whether current sales increased or the comparison base fell. Looking across several periods at actual sales, market share and purchase costs provides a firmer basis for judging demand. The earlier sequential improvement cannot simply be extrapolated into continuing growth in future quarters.

## When do exports become overseas demand?

Regional divergence can also change manufacturers’ dependence on foreign markets. The IEA’s July report found that Chinese electric-car exports grew considerably faster in the first half than overseas sales of those vehicles. It estimated that more than one million vehicles exported during its preceding 18-month observation window had not yet been registered as sales in destination-market statistics.[^2]

The two records have a timing gap, partly explained by shipping and delivery. The IEA nevertheless judged that the size of the difference also suggested inventory accumulation in some destination markets.[^2] This was a midyear estimate, not a measurement of inventory at the end of September. Nor can the whole difference be classified as unsellable vehicles.

It raises an important operating question: whether overseas shipments can sustainably improve factory utilisation depends on final purchases in destination markets. Vehicles remaining in distribution channels can bring discounting, storage and working-capital costs that erode the gains from growth. That is an operating risk inferred from the distinction between shipments and sales, not a claim that a particular company is already making losses.

## What evidence is needed next?

Assessing the EV industry requires three questions to be answered together: are consumers buying more vehicles; are EVs gaining share from other powertrains; and is final demand absorbing increased production?

The IEA’s purchase-price research also shows why falling battery costs need not translate proportionately into lower retail prices. Vehicle size, specification, product mix and manufacturers’ pricing decisions all affect the outcome.[^6] Manufacturers and suppliers therefore need to examine demand structure and profitability conditions together. Consistent signals from volumes, model mix, prices and inventory turnover offer a more reliable way to distinguish temporary statistical fluctuations from lasting changes in operating conditions.
