U.S. producer prices picked up in September, and the pressure showed up in energy goods, in distribution, and in the inputs businesses buy. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on October 15 that the final-demand Producer Price Index, or PPI, rose a seasonally adjusted 1.3% in September, after 0.5% in August and 0.2% in July. The unadjusted annual increase widened from 5.6% in August to 6.3%. Final-demand goods prices rose 2.6% over the month. Services prices increased 0.8%.[^1]

The headline is only the start. Energy provided the main impetus on the goods side, and a substantial part of the services increase came from the trade margins received by wholesalers and retailers. Final-demand prices still rose after food, energy and trade services were taken out. Goods prices, distribution services and the prices that remain all have to be read, and any pass-through after that still has to be shown.

## Energy accounted for more than four-fifths of the goods increase

Energy in final demand rose a seasonally adjusted 10.8% in September, after 4.5% in August. BLS attributed more than 80% of the month’s increase in final-demand goods to energy. Gasoline prices rose 20.3% and accounted for half of that goods increase. The denominator in both contribution statements is final-demand goods. The overall final-demand PPI also includes services and construction.[^1]

Away from energy, final-demand food prices rose 1.2% over the month, and goods excluding food and energy rose 0.3%. That latter category had increased 0.4% in August. The overall goods increase accelerated, and the monthly pace for nonfood, nonenergy goods eased slightly. On an unadjusted annual basis, final-demand goods rose 9.8%, with energy up 35.4%, goods excluding food and energy up 5.1%, and food unchanged from a year earlier.[^1]

The time horizon changes the reading. The monthly food rebound did not take prices above their year-earlier level. A smaller monthly increase in nonenergy goods does not erase the price increases accumulated over the previous year. A purchasing business has to match the data to its own inputs and contract periods. The 2.6% overall goods increase is not a uniform adjustment to every purchase.

## Nearly half of the services increase came from trade margins

Final-demand services prices rose a seasonally adjusted 0.8% in September, following 0.2% in August. Trade services rose 1.4% and, according to BLS, accounted for nearly half of the services increase. Transportation and warehousing services rose 3.4%. Services excluding trade, transportation and warehousing increased 0.3%. Prices were changing in goods and in distribution. The statistical meaning of those movements is not the same.[^1]

A trade-services PPI is a wholesale or retail margin: the selling price of a product minus the current price of acquiring a replacement of the same product. The margin is the price of the distribution service. It is not the full price on the shelf, and it is not net profit after wages, rent and other expenses. Selling prices and replenishment prices can move on their own, so the margin index can rise sharply even when the shelf price barely changes.[^2][^3]

The release gives a concrete case. Margins for machinery and vehicle wholesaling rose 3.9% and contributed more than 20% of the monthly services increase. Table 2 identifies that detailed series as not seasonally adjusted. The same release reported declining margins for automotive fuels and lubricants retailing, even as gasoline producer prices rose sharply. Higher fuel prices and a narrower margin on selling fuel can coexist. Product prices and the return to distribution have to be looked at separately.[^1]

That changes how a supply chain should be read. Prices received by upstream sellers, transportation-service prices and distribution margins each need their own check. Calling all of them “wholesale prices” obscures who is receiving higher prices, who faces higher acquisition costs, and where the margin between them is changing.

## Excluding three volatile categories still leaves a 0.5% monthly increase

Final demand excluding food, energy and trade services rose a seasonally adjusted 0.5% in September, up from 0.3% in August. Its unadjusted annual increase widened from 4.8% to 5.0%. By setting aside both energy goods and trade margins, the measure offers another view of the prices that remain. Its coverage differs from the measure that excludes only food and energy, so anyone referring to “core PPI.” has to say which exclusions they mean.[^1]

The pressure did not vanish once those three categories were removed. Removing them does not establish that energy caused all the remaining increases. Transportation firms, manufacturers and other service providers may face energy costs, but their pricing also depends on contracts, competition and demand. Taking out the direct energy category does not take out energy costs that may be embedded in other prices. The release does not separately identify those indirect effects.

## Upstream inputs rose together, but the increases cannot be added along the chain

Intermediate demand turns to the inputs businesses buy for production. In September, seasonally adjusted prices rose 2.5% for processed intermediate goods, 8.4% for unprocessed intermediate goods and 0.9% for intermediate-demand services. Unadjusted, the annual increases were 14.1%, 21.5% and 6.3%. The release has price information beyond final demand.[^1]

Upstream, energy again supplied most of the goods inflation. Processed energy goods rose 10.2% on the month and explained more than 80% of the increase in processed intermediate goods. Unprocessed energy materials rose 22.2% and likewise explained more than 80% of the increase in unprocessed goods. Processed materials excluding food and energy still rose 0.5%. Unprocessed nonfood materials excluding energy rose 2.5%. The changes reached beyond the energy categories.[^1]

They are not a cost bill to be added stage by stage. BLS allocates product weights by buyer and by use, and the same commodity-price series can enter indexes for different uses. Households buy gasoline. Businesses use it as an input. Intermediate demand also leaves capital investment out. Buying equipment and buying routine production materials occupy different places in the final-demand/intermediate-demand system.[^1][^2]

The 8.4% increase in unprocessed intermediate goods cannot simply be added to the 2.5% processed-goods increase, and then to the 1.3% final-demand increase. They are price changes for different baskets with different weights. They flag links in the chain that merit attention. They do not, on their own, establish that a particular cost increase has already been passed fully to the next buyer.

## Purchasing and operating decisions need item-level evidence

Business inputs did not all become more expensive at one rate. Private capital equipment within final demand rose a seasonally adjusted 0.3% on the month and an unadjusted 4.0% on the year, much less than energy rose in the month. A firm that depends on fuel-intensive transportation is looking at a different direct price signal from a firm planning to buy equipment.[^1]

For companies with contracts already in place, the relevant details are which index the contract uses, when repricing occurs and which inputs it covers. The release’s technical note explains that long-term contract escalation generally uses unadjusted data, while seasonally adjusted monthly changes are principally useful for examining trends. Whether the 1.3% headline PPI increase applies to an existing supply contract depends on those provisions.[^1]

Operating results also need quantities and expenditure. A higher unit price received by a seller does not automatically mean higher sales volumes, revenue or net profit. Wider trade margins likewise do not establish better profits after all expenses. If input-price increases persist and companies cannot offset them through contracts or operating changes, cost pressure may increase. If demand limits pass-through, the effect may fall on margins. That reading follows from what the measures represent. The release itself does not estimate changes in profits that businesses have already realized.

## Preliminary figures and revisions shape the next assessment

September is marked preliminary. Some May-through-August figures were revised to take in late reports and corrections from respondents. A PPI generally stays open to revision for four months after the originally scheduled publication. Seasonally adjusted series can also change for the previous five years when the seasonal factors are re-estimated. A comparison with an earlier month should use figures from the same vintage of the release.[^1]

One boundary sits underneath all of this. The PPI measures prices received by domestic U.S. producers. Final demand takes in personal consumption, capital investment, government purchases and exports. It is not a household shopping basket, and it is not a complete input-cost index for every company. Coverage and pricing methods differ from consumer-price measures. September’s 6.3% annual increase cannot be presented as the rise in household living costs.[^1][^2]

Energy increases may persist, or they may not. Monthly increases excluding food, energy and trade services may stay elevated. Upstream prices and downstream trade margins may adjust relative to one another in any number of ways. Those are the next tests. The release schedules October PPI for November 13 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time.[^1] Until the next data and business disclosures arrive, September’s clear conclusion is that producer-price pressure expanded, with both energy and distribution margins contributing. Which part of the chain absorbs the costs still needs more specific evidence.
